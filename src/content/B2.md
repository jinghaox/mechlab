The next lesson describes a system's behaviour by a handful of points called *poles*, and those points are complex numbers. This lesson gives you what you need: what a complex number is, how to picture it, and the one formula, Euler's, that connects complex numbers to oscillation.

## Why they exist

Try to solve $s^2 + 4s + 100 = 0$ with the quadratic formula:

$$s = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 400}}{2} = -2 \pm \frac{\sqrt{-384}}{2}$$

There is a negative number under the square root, so no real number works. Rather than give up, mathematicians defined a new number $i$ with the property

$$i^2 = -1$$

Then $\sqrt{-384} = \sqrt{384}\,i \approx 19.6\,i$, and the two solutions are $s = -2 \pm 9.8\,i$. That equation is not a random example: it belongs to a mass-spring-damper, and you will meet these exact numbers again in lesson 1.5.

Engineers usually write $j$ instead of $i$, because $i$ is already taken for electric current. Python uses `j` as well. This course uses $j$ from here on.

## A number with two parts

A **complex number** has a real part and an imaginary part:

$$z = a + jb$$

Picture it as a point on a plane, the **complex plane**: $a$ along the horizontal (real) axis and $b$ along the vertical (imaginary) axis. Ordinary real numbers are the points on the horizontal axis.

Arithmetic works the way you would guess, treating $j$ like a variable and replacing $j^2$ by $-1$ whenever it appears:

$$(3 + 4j) + (1 - 2j) = 4 + 2j$$

$$(3 + 4j)(1 - 2j) = 3 - 6j + 4j - 8j^2 = 11 - 2j$$

The **conjugate** of $a + jb$ is $a - jb$, its mirror image across the real axis. The complex roots of an equation with real coefficients always come in conjugate pairs, like $-2 \pm 9.8j$ above.

## Magnitude and angle

A point on a plane can also be described by its distance from the origin and its direction. For $z = a + jb$:

- the **magnitude** is $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$
- the **angle** (or phase) is the angle from the positive real axis, $\angle z = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$

For $z = 3 + 4j$ the magnitude is 5 and the angle is 53.1°.

This polar description makes multiplication simple:

> To multiply two complex numbers, **multiply their magnitudes and add their angles**.

So multiplying by a complex number *scales and rotates*. In particular $j$ has magnitude 1 and angle 90°, so multiplying by $j$ rotates a point by a quarter turn: $(3 + 4j)\cdot j = -4 + 3j$. Do it twice and you have rotated by 180°, which is the same as multiplying by $-1$. That is the geometric meaning of $j^2 = -1$.

## Euler's formula

Here is the formula that makes complex numbers indispensable in engineering:

$$e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$$

The number $e^{j\theta}$ is the point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$. It has magnitude 1 always. As $\theta$ increases, the point travels around the circle; its horizontal position traces a cosine and its vertical position a sine.

Now make the angle grow with time, $\theta = \omega t$. Then $e^{j\omega t}$ is a point circling at $\omega$ radians per second, and its real part, $\cos\omega t$, is an **oscillation**. A complex exponential is a compact way to write a sine wave.

## The payoff: one number describes a whole response

Combine a real and an imaginary part in the exponent. Using the rule $e^{a+b} = e^a e^b$:

$$e^{(\sigma + j\omega)t} = e^{\sigma t}\left(\cos\omega t + j\sin\omega t\right)$$

Look at what each part of the number $s = \sigma + j\omega$ does:

| Part of $s$ | Effect on $e^{st}$ |
|---|---|
| real part $\sigma < 0$ | the amplitude **decays**, with time constant $1/\lvert\sigma\rvert$ |
| real part $\sigma > 0$ | the amplitude **grows** without limit |
| imaginary part $\omega$ | it **oscillates** at $\omega$ rad/s |
| imaginary part $= 0$ | no oscillation, a plain exponential |

So $s = -2 + 9.8j$ means "an oscillation at 9.8 rad/s whose amplitude decays like $e^{-2t}$". That is the ringing of an underdamped system from lesson 1.2, packed into one complex number. The next lesson shows that every linear system has a small set of such numbers, its poles, and that reading them off the complex plane tells you how the system behaves.

## In Python

```python
import cmath
import numpy as np

z = 3 + 4j
abs(z)                        # 5.0
np.degrees(cmath.phase(z))    # 53.13
z * 1j                        # (-4+3j), rotated by 90 degrees
cmath.exp(1j * np.pi)         # -1, to within rounding
np.roots([1, 4, 100])         # [-2.+9.798j, -2.-9.798j]
```

=== frc ===

A swerve module's wheel direction and a robot's heading are both "points on the unit circle", and WPILib's `Rotation2d` stores exactly that: a cosine and a sine, which are the real and imaginary parts of $e^{j\theta}$. When you call `rotateBy` to combine two rotations, it is multiplying two complex numbers, adding their angles. That is also why `Rotation2d` never has trouble with wrap-around at ±180°, while a bare angle in degrees does.

=== summary ===

- $j^2 = -1$. A complex number $a + jb$ is a point on a plane.
- Magnitude $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, angle $\operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$. Multiplying multiplies magnitudes and adds angles.
- Euler's formula: $e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$, a point on the unit circle.
- $e^{(\sigma + j\omega)t}$ oscillates at $\omega$ with an amplitude that decays ($\sigma < 0$) or grows ($\sigma > 0$).
- Complex roots of real equations come in conjugate pairs.
