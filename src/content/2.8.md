Open the firmware of any servo drive, drone or industrial robot and you will not find one PID loop per axis. You will find **loops inside loops**: a position controller that commands a velocity, a velocity controller that commands a current, and a current controller that commands a voltage. This structure is called **cascaded control**, and it is the standard architecture for motion control. This lesson explains why, and how to tune one.

## The structure

A motor-driven axis has a natural chain of cause and effect:

$$\text{voltage} \;\to\; \text{current} \;\to\; \text{torque} \;\to\; \text{velocity} \;\to\; \text{position}$$

Each arrow is roughly an integration or a first-order lag. A cascade puts a controller at each stage, with each one's output serving as the **setpoint** of the next one in:

| Loop | Measures | Outputs | Typical rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position (outer) | angle | velocity command | 50 Hz – 1 kHz |
| Velocity (middle) | speed | current (torque) command | 1 – 4 kHz |
| Current (inner) | motor current | voltage (PWM duty) | 10 – 40 kHz |

The inner loops are faster than the outer ones, and for a simple reason: they control faster physics. Current responds in under a millisecond; position takes a tenth of a second.

## Why not just one loop?

A single PID from position error to voltage can work. The cascade wins on four counts.

**1. Disturbances are caught early.** A load torque first changes the velocity, and only later, after integration, the position. A velocity loop sees the disturbance and fights it before it has had time to become a position error. A single position loop must wait for the position error to build up.

**2. Limits can be enforced.** The output of each loop is the setpoint of the next, so you can simply **clamp** it. Clamp the velocity command and the axis never exceeds a safe speed. Clamp the current command and the motor and driver are protected, however large the position error. In a single loop there is no signal that *is* the velocity command, so there is nothing to clamp.

**3. Each loop faces a simple plant.** A well-tuned inner loop makes its output follow its setpoint. From outside, it looks like a gain of 1 with a small delay. So the velocity loop does not have to deal with the electrical dynamics; it sees "a device that produces the torque I ask for". The position loop sees "a device that moves at the speed I ask for", which is a pure integrator, and an integrator under P control is first-order and cannot overshoot. A hard third-order design problem becomes three easy first-order ones.

**4. Nonlinearities are contained.** Friction, back-EMF and driver nonlinearity act inside the inner loops and are corrected there. They are largely invisible to the outer loop.

## The tuning rule: inside out, separated in speed

Point 3 only holds if each inner loop is much faster than the one around it. The rule is

> Each loop should have **5 to 10 times the bandwidth** of the loop outside it.

Tune from the inside out:

1. **Current loop first**, with the rotor locked or the outer loops open. Usually PI.
2. **Velocity loop next**, with the position loop open. Usually PI: the integral term rejects constant load torques.
3. **Position loop last.** Usually P alone. The integrator from velocity to position already gives zero steady-state error, and the velocity loop's integral handles loads.

If the bandwidths are too close together the loops interact, and the "inner loop looks like 1" simplification fails. The separation requirement is also the cascade's main cost: the outer loop is necessarily several times slower than the innermost one, so the fastest loop's sample rate and sensor quality set a ceiling for the whole axis.

## A comparison

The lab controls an axis with a 2 N·m torque limit that must not exceed 5 rad/s, moving 10 rad and then taking a load torque of 0.5 N·m:

| | Peak speed | Overshoot | Arrival | Dip under load | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single PID | 43 rad/s | 3.2% | 0.99 s | 0.103 rad | 1.18 s |
| Cascade | 5.2 rad/s | 0% | 2.07 s | 0.014 rad | 0.35 s |

Read this table carefully, because it is not a simple win.

- The single loop arrives in half the time, by running at **43 rad/s**, more than eight times the speed limit. It has no way of knowing the limit exists.
- The cascade accelerates to 5 rad/s, cruises at exactly that speed, and decelerates into the target with no overshoot. Its arrival time of about 2 s is the physical minimum for a 10 rad move at 5 rad/s. The motion it produces, accelerate, cruise, decelerate, is the trapezoidal velocity profile, emerging here from nothing more than a clamp.
- Under the load torque, the cascade's position dips 7 times less and recovers 3 times sooner. The velocity loop's integral term absorbs the disturbance.

A fair caveat: a single loop tuned more aggressively would reject the disturbance better than this one does. What it could not do, at any tuning, is respect the speed limit.

## Where the limits go

Because each clamp sits between two loops, saturation needs the same care as in lesson 2.6. When the velocity command is clamped, the position error is large and persistent. That is harmless here because the position loop is P-only and has no integral to wind up. But the velocity loop's integrator **can** wind up while the torque is saturated, so it needs anti-windup. In the lab code, the line that skips integration while `u != u_sat` is that protection.

=== frc ===

Modern FRC motor controllers are cascades you configure. A TalonFX or SPARK MAX in position mode runs the position loop on board at 1 kHz or faster; with Motion Magic or MAXMotion it also generates the speed-limited, acceleration-limited profile that the cascade in this lesson produced with a clamp. Torque-current (FOC) control modes expose the innermost current loop directly. A common and effective split is the one this lesson recommends: let the motor controller close the fast velocity or current loop next to the sensor, and run only the slow outer loop, such as a vision-based aiming loop or a trajectory follower, on the roboRIO at 50 Hz. The 20 ms outer loop is then comfortably 10 or more times slower than the inner one.

=== summary ===

- Cascade: position → velocity → current, each loop's output being the next loop's setpoint.
- Benefits: early disturbance rejection, enforceable limits at every level, a simple plant for each loop, contained nonlinearities.
- Each inner loop needs 5–10 times the bandwidth of the loop around it.
- Tune inside out: current (PI), then velocity (PI), then position (P).
- Clamping the velocity command yields an accelerate–cruise–decelerate motion automatically.
- Any loop with an integrator and a clamped output needs anti-windup.
