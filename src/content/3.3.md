Orientation is half the story. A body also has a **position**, and the two together are called its **pose**. This lesson combines a rotation and a translation into a single 4×4 matrix, the **homogeneous transform**, and shows how chaining such matrices answers the question robotics asks constantly: *I know where this is relative to that, and that relative to something else; where is it relative to me?*

## From one frame to another

Frame $B$ sits somewhere in frame $A$: its origin is at position $p$ (in $A$'s coordinates) and its axes are rotated by $R$. A point with coordinates $x_B$ in frame $B$ has, in frame $A$,

$$x_A = R\,x_B + p$$

Rotate into $A$'s orientation, then shift by where $B$'s origin is. This is correct, but chaining two of these gives $R_1(R_2 x + p_2) + p_1$, and three becomes unmanageable.

## The homogeneous transform

The trick is to append a 1 to every point, making it a 4-vector, and pack $R$ and $p$ into a 4×4 matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_A \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0\ 0\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{T} \begin{bmatrix} x_B \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply it out and the top three rows give $Rx_B + p$, as before, and the bottom row gives $1 = 1$. Nothing new has happened mathematically, but "rotate and shift" is now a single matrix multiplication, so a chain of them is simply a product.

A notation that prevents most mistakes: write $T_{AB}$ for "the pose of frame $B$ expressed in frame $A$". The same matrix converts coordinates from $B$ into $A$:

$$x_A = T_{AB}\,x_B$$

Read the subscripts like dominoes. Adjacent ones must match, and they cancel:

$$T_{AC} = T_{AB}\,T_{BC}$$

If the inner subscripts do not match, the expression is wrong. This one habit catches a large share of frame bugs before any code runs.

## The inverse

To go the other way, from $A$ into $B$, you need $T_{BA} = T_{AB}^{-1}$. Because the rotation block is a rotation matrix, there is a closed form:

$$T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^T p \\ 0\ 0\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Use this, not a general-purpose matrix inverse. It is faster, and it is exact: the result is guaranteed to be a valid transform.

## A worked example

A robot base sits on a table. A camera is mounted above the table, looking straight down. The camera sees a cube. The robot needs the cube's position **in its own base frame** in order to reach for it.

What is known:

- $T_{WB}$, the base in the world: at $(0.5, 0.2, 0)$, turned 90° about $z$.
- $T_{WC}$, the camera in the world: at $(0.5, 0.6, 1.0)$, flipped 180° about $x$ so that its $z$ axis points down.
- The cube in the camera frame: $(0.10, -0.05, 0.95)$.

What is wanted is the cube in frame $B$. Line up the dominoes:

$$x_B = T_{BW}\,T_{WC}\,x_C = T_{WB}^{-1}\,T_{WC}\,x_C$$

The lab computes it:

| Frame | Cube position |
|---|---|
| camera | $(0.10,\ -0.05,\ 0.95)$ |
| world | $(0.60,\ 0.65,\ 0.05)$ |
| robot base | $(0.45,\ -0.10,\ 0.05)$ |

The same physical cube has three different sets of coordinates. None is more correct than the others; each answers "where is it?" for a different observer. A number without a frame is meaningless, and a surprising fraction of real robot bugs amount to a position being used in the wrong frame.

## Order matters, again

Transforms compose like rotations: on the right for motion relative to the body's own frame, on the left for motion relative to the fixed frame. "Rotate 90° about $z$, then move 1 m along $x$" depends on *whose* $x$:

- $T_\text{trans}\,T_\text{rot}$ sends the origin to $(1, 0, 0)$: moved along the fixed $x$.
- $T_\text{rot}\,T_\text{trans}$ sends the origin to $(0, 1, 0)$: moved along the rotated $x$.

## Points and directions

The appended 1 has a meaning. A **point** has a location, so translation affects it: use a 1. A **direction**, such as a velocity, a force or a camera's viewing axis, has no location, and shifting the frame does not change it: use a **0** instead, and the translation column drops out automatically.

$$\text{point: } \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\qquad \text{direction: } \begin{bmatrix} v \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

In the example, the camera's viewing direction $(0, 0, 1)$ becomes $(0, 0, -1)$ in the world frame: straight down, as intended.

## Frame chains in a robot

A robot is a chain of frames, one per link, each located relative to the previous one by a transform that depends on a joint angle:

$$T_{\text{world, tool}} = T_{\text{world, base}}\;T_{01}(q_1)\;T_{12}(q_2)\,\cdots\,T_{n-1,n}(q_n)\;T_{n,\text{tool}}$$

Computing that product for given joint angles is **forward kinematics**, the subject of the next lesson. MuJoCo does exactly this at every step: for each body it stores the result as `data.xpos` (the position) and `data.xmat` (the rotation matrix), both expressed in the world frame.

=== frc ===

WPILib's `Transform3d` and `Pose3d` are homogeneous transforms with friendlier names, and the standard AprilTag localization is the domino rule applied once. The field layout gives the tag's pose in the field, $T_{\text{field, tag}}$. The camera measures the tag in its own frame, $T_{\text{camera, tag}}$. Your robot configuration gives the camera's mounting pose, $T_{\text{robot, camera}}$. The robot's pose on the field is then

$$T_{\text{field, robot}} = T_{\text{field, tag}}\;T_{\text{camera, tag}}^{-1}\;T_{\text{robot, camera}}^{-1}$$

Check the subscripts: field–tag, tag–camera, camera–robot. PhotonVision and Limelight perform this chain for you, which is why an error in the camera's mounting transform shows up as a constant offset in every pose estimate.

=== summary ===

- Pose = orientation + position. $x_A = R\,x_B + p$.
- Homogeneous transform: $T = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, applied to $\begin{bmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
- $T_{AB}$ is frame $B$ seen from $A$; it maps $B$-coordinates to $A$-coordinates. Chain rule: $T_{AC} = T_{AB}T_{BC}$.
- $T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^T p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
- Points take a 1 in the fourth slot; directions take a 0.
- A coordinate is meaningless without its frame.

=== quiz ===

? You have $T_{WA}$ and $T_{WB}$, the poses of frames $A$ and $B$ in the world. Which expression gives the pose of $B$ as seen from $A$?
- $T_{WA}\,T_{WB}$
- $T_{WB}\,T_{WA}^{-1}$
* $T_{WA}^{-1}\,T_{WB}$
- $T_{WB}^{-1}\,T_{WA}$
> $T_{AB} = T_{AW}\,T_{WB}$ and $T_{AW} = T_{WA}^{-1}$. The inner subscripts ($W$) match and cancel.

? A transform has rotation $R$ and translation $p$. What is the translation part of its inverse?
- $-p$
- $p$
* $-R^T p$
- $R\,p$
> The inverse must undo the shift in the *rotated-back* frame, so the translation is $-R^Tp$, not simply $-p$.

? You transform a velocity vector from the tool frame to the base frame using a full 4×4 transform with a 1 in the fourth slot. What is wrong with the result?
- Nothing
- The rotation is applied twice
* The translation is wrongly added to the velocity
- The velocity is reversed
> A velocity is a direction, not a point. It should be transformed with a 0 in the fourth slot so that only the rotation acts on it.
