Most arms have no closed-form inverse kinematics. The general-purpose alternative is to **iterate**: start from a guess, see how far the hand is from the target, use the Jacobian to work out a joint correction, and repeat. This lesson builds that solver in three versions, shows why the obvious one fails near singularities, and ends with a five-joint arm drawing a circle in MuJoCo.

## The idea

Near the current configuration the Jacobian relates small changes:

$$\Delta x \approx J(q)\,\Delta q$$

You want the hand to move by the error $e = x_\text{target} - x(q)$. So solve for a $\Delta q$ that produces it, take that step, and repeat, because the Jacobian is only accurate nearby:

1. Compute the tip position $x(q)$ and the error $e$.
2. If $\|e\|$ is small enough, stop.
3. Compute $J(q)$ and solve $J\,\Delta q = e$ in some sense.
4. Update $q \leftarrow q + \Delta q$ and go to step 1.

This is Newton's method applied to the forward kinematics. Everything interesting is in step 3, because $J$ is generally not a square, invertible matrix. The five-joint arm of the lab has three position coordinates and five joints: $J$ is 3×5.

## Version 1: Jacobian transpose

The simplest choice avoids solving anything:

$$\Delta q = \alpha\,J^T e$$

Recall $\tau = J^TF$. This treats the error as a spring force pulling the hand toward the target and moves each joint in the direction that force would turn it. It is gradient descent on the squared error $\tfrac12\|e\|^2$.

It is cheap and never blows up, but it is slow. In the lab it is still 4.6 mm from the target after 200 iterations. Gradient descent takes steps in proportion to the slope, and the slope fades as the target gets close.

## Version 2: the pseudoinverse

To solve $J\,\Delta q = e$ properly when $J$ is not square, use the **Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse**, written $J^+$:

$$\Delta q = J^+ e$$

For an arm with more joints than task coordinates, many $\Delta q$ produce the same hand motion, and $J^+ = J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}$ picks the one with the **smallest joint motion**. In NumPy it is `np.linalg.pinv(J)`.

This is a true Newton step and converges very quickly near the solution: **4 iterations** in the lab, against more than 200.

Its weakness is the one lesson 3.5 warned about. Near a singularity, $JJ^T$ is nearly non-invertible and the computed $\Delta q$ becomes enormous. Ask for an unreachable target and the arm stretches out fully, which *is* a singularity, and then thrashes: in the lab the joints are still jumping by 11.5° per iteration after 100 iterations, never settling.

## Version 3: damped least squares

The fix is to stop demanding an exact solution. Minimize a combination of the remaining error and the size of the step:

$$\min_{\Delta q}\ \|J\,\Delta q - e\|^2 + \lambda^2\|\Delta q\|^2$$

The second term penalizes large joint motions. The solution is

$$\Delta q = J^T\left(JJ^T + \lambda^2 I\right)^{-1} e$$

This is **damped least squares** (DLS), also called the Levenberg–Marquardt method. The only change from the pseudoinverse is the added $\lambda^2 I$, which keeps the matrix invertible everywhere.

The damping factor $\lambda$ sets the trade-off:

- Far from singularities, $JJ^T$ is much larger than $\lambda^2 I$ and DLS behaves like the pseudoinverse: fast.
- Near a singularity, the damping takes over, and the step in the nearly lost direction is scaled down smoothly toward zero, where the pseudoinverse would have scaled it up toward infinity.

In the lab DLS reaches the ordinary target in 5 iterations, one more than the pseudoinverse. For the unreachable target it stops moving, with the arm stretched toward the target and the tip 24.5 cm short, which is as close as that arm can physically get. A sensible answer to an impossible question is what you want from a solver that runs unattended.

## Making it robust in practice

A solver that works on real problems needs a few more safeguards. The lab's `solve_ik` has all of them.

- **Limit the step.** The Jacobian is a local approximation. Cap $\Delta q$, here at 0.2 rad per iteration, so one step never leaves the region where it is valid.
- **Limit the error.** If the target is far away, aim for a point a few centimetres along the way. Otherwise a large error multiplied by a nearly singular inverse still gives a wild step.
- **Respect joint limits.** Clip $q$ to the joint ranges after each update.
- **Start well.** The method finds *a* solution near the starting guess, not all solutions, and from a poor start it can stall. Never start from a singular pose: with the lab's arm pointing straight up, the Jacobian has rank 1, and the solver has almost no information about which way to bend.

## Following a path: warm starts

To trace a path, solve IK at each point along it and **use the previous point's answer as the next starting guess**. Consecutive targets are close together, so the previous solution is an excellent guess.

The lab traces a 12 cm circle in 120 points:

- about **2 iterations per point**,
- a largest error of 0.09 mm,
- joint angles that change by at most 1.3° from one point to the next.

Warm starting also solves the branch problem of lesson 3.6 automatically. Because each solve starts from the last answer, the solution stays on one branch and the joints move continuously.

## Redundancy and the null space

Five joints for a three-dimensional position task leaves two degrees of freedom to spare. Motions of the joints that leave the hand exactly where it is form the **null space** of $J$. One such motion is obvious in this arm: the wrist roll joint spins the tool about its own axis, and the tip, which lies on that axis, does not move. Its column in $J$ is zero.

The spare freedom is useful. A general solution is

$$\Delta q = J^+ e + \left(I - J^+J\right)\Delta q_0$$

where $\Delta q_0$ is *any* joint motion you would like, such as "move toward the middle of the joint ranges" or "keep the elbow away from the table". The matrix $(I - J^+J)$ projects it into the null space, so it is carried out only to the extent that it does not disturb the hand. This is how a redundant arm reaches around obstacles while its hand holds still.

## Adding orientation

Position alone used three rows of the Jacobian. To control orientation too, stack the angular rows `jacr` under `jacp` and add an orientation error to $e$. That error should be a **rotation vector** (lesson 3.2): the axis and angle of the rotation that takes the current orientation to the target. Subtracting Euler angles does not work.

A five-joint arm cannot reach an arbitrary position **and** an arbitrary orientation, since that takes six degrees of freedom. It can control position plus two orientation angles, which is enough to keep a gripper pointing straight down.

## Kinematics is not the whole story

The solver gives joint angles that would put the hand on the circle if the joints were exactly there. The viewer script sends those angles to position-controlled motors in a real simulation with gravity, and the tip then tracks the circle with an average error of about 7 mm: the P-only servos sag under the arm's own weight, as the arm of lesson 2.2 did. Closing that gap needs the *dynamics* of the arm, which is Module 4.

=== frc ===

For a two- or three-joint FRC mechanism the closed-form IK of lesson 3.6 is the right tool, and numerical IK would be overkill: it is slower, it finds only one solution, and it needs a good starting guess. The method of this lesson becomes necessary once you move beyond competition mechanisms to arms with five or more joints, such as the SO-101 this course is heading toward. The IK solvers in the tools you will meet there, such as those in MoveIt for ROS 2 and the Jacobian-based solvers written for MuJoCo, are refinements of the same loop: compute the error, compute the Jacobian, take a damped step, repeat. Having written one yourself, you will know what their damping, tolerance and iteration-limit settings mean, and what has probably happened when one of them fails to converge.

=== summary ===

- Numerical IK: repeat $q \leftarrow q + \Delta q$ with $J\,\Delta q \approx e$ until the error is small.
- Transpose, $\Delta q = \alpha J^Te$: simple and safe, but slow.
- Pseudoinverse, $\Delta q = J^+e$: fast, but unstable near singularities.
- Damped least squares, $\Delta q = J^T(JJ^T + \lambda^2I)^{-1}e$: fast away from singularities and well-behaved near them.
- Limit the step and the error, enforce joint limits, and avoid singular starting poses.
- Warm-start each point of a path from the previous solution.
- Spare joints give a null space, $(I - J^+J)\Delta q_0$, for secondary goals.
