Forward kinematics relates joint **angles** to the hand's **position**. Differentiate it and you get the relation between joint **speeds** and the hand's **velocity**. That relation is a matrix, the **Jacobian**, and it is the most useful single object in robot kinematics. The same matrix converts forces at the hand into torques at the joints, tells you where the arm loses mobility, and drives the inverse kinematics of lesson 3.7.

## Definition

Write the forward kinematics for position as $x = f(q)$. By the chain rule,

$$\dot{x} = J(q)\,\dot{q}, \qquad J = \frac{\partial f}{\partial q}$$

$J$ has one row per task-space coordinate and one column per joint. Its entries are partial derivatives of the tip position with respect to the joint angles, so they depend on the arm's current configuration $q$: **the Jacobian changes as the arm moves.**

For the two-link arm, differentiating

$$x = L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12} \qquad z = L_1 s_1 + L_2 s_{12}$$

(with the shorthand $c_1 = \cos q_1$, $s_{12} = \sin(q_1+q_2)$, and so on) gives

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12} & -L_2 s_{12} \\ L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12} & L_2 c_{12} \end{bmatrix}$$

## Reading the columns

Column $j$ is the velocity of the tip when joint $j$ turns at 1 rad/s and every other joint is held still.

That gives a geometric way to write the Jacobian of any arm with rotary joints, without differentiating anything. A point at position $r$ from a joint's axis $\hat{\omega}$, carried around that axis at 1 rad/s, moves with velocity $\hat{\omega} \times r$. So

$$J_j = \hat{\omega}_j \times (p_\text{tip} - p_j)$$

where $p_j$ is any point on joint $j$'s axis. The tip's velocity from one joint is perpendicular to the line from that joint to the tip, and proportional to its length. Check it against the matrix above: the second column, $(-L_2 s_{12},\ L_2 c_{12})$, is a vector of length $L_2$ perpendicular to link 2.

This is how MuJoCo computes Jacobians. In the lab the analytic formula, a finite-difference estimate and `mujoco.mj_jacSite` all agree. At $q = (40°, 65°)$:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} -0.434 & -0.242 \\ 0.165 & -0.065 \end{bmatrix}$$

and joint speeds of $(1, -0.5)$ rad/s move the tip at $(-0.314, 0.198)$ m/s.

For a full 3-D arm the Jacobian has six rows: three for the tip's linear velocity and three for its angular velocity. `mj_jacSite` returns them as two 3×n blocks, `jacp` and `jacr`.

## The same matrix maps forces

Suppose the tip pushes on the world with force $F$. What torques must the joints supply?

Use conservation of power. The power delivered at the joints, $\tau^T\dot{q}$, must equal the power delivered at the tip, $F^T\dot{x}$. Substituting $\dot{x} = J\dot{q}$:

$$\tau^T\dot{q} = F^T J\,\dot{q} \quad\text{for every } \dot{q} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau = J^T F$$

The **transpose** of the Jacobian maps tip forces to joint torques. In the lab, holding a 10 N downward load at the tip in that configuration requires joint torques of $(1.65, -0.65)$ N·m.

This relation is used in two directions. Given a desired force at the hand, it tells you what torques to command, which is the basis of force control in Module 4. Given a load at the hand, it tells you how large the motors must be.

Velocity and force are *dual*: $J$ carries velocities from joints to tip, and $J^T$ carries forces from tip to joints. A configuration where a small joint motion gives a large tip motion is, for the same reason, one where a small tip force needs only a small joint torque to resist. A long lever is fast and weak; a short one is slow and strong.

## Singularities

To move the tip with a desired velocity you need the joint speeds, which means inverting the relation: $\dot{q} = J^{-1}\dot{x}$. That works only when $J$ is invertible. Configurations where it is not are called **singularities**.

For the two-link arm the determinant is remarkably simple:

$$\det J = L_1 L_2 \sin q_2$$

It is zero when $q_2 = 0$ (arm stretched out straight) or $q_2 = 180°$ (arm folded back on itself). It does not depend on $q_1$ at all: turning the whole arm about the shoulder does not change its shape.

At a singularity three things happen at once.

**1. A direction of motion is lost.** With the arm straight, both columns of $J$ point the same way, perpendicular to the arm. Whatever the joints do, the tip can only move sideways. It cannot move outward, because it is at full reach, and, less obviously, it cannot move straight inward either, not instantaneously.

**2. Nearby, joint speeds explode.** Close to the singularity the lost direction is merely *expensive*. The lab asks for a modest 0.1 m/s straight outward:

| Elbow angle | $\det J$ | Joint speed required |
|---|---|---|
| 90° | 0.0750 | 30°/s |
| 45° | 0.0530 | 55°/s |
| 10° | 0.0130 | 241°/s |
| 2° | 0.0026 | 1204°/s |
| 0.5° | 0.0007 | 4815°/s |

No motor can deliver 4815°/s. A controller that inverts the Jacobian blindly will command it anyway.

**3. The arm becomes infinitely strong in that direction.** By duality, a force along the lost direction needs no joint torque to resist: $J^TF = 0$. A straight arm carries a load along its length through its structure, not its motors. You use this every time you lock your knees to stand or carry a heavy bag with a straight arm.

Singularities fall into two kinds. **Boundary singularities** occur at the edge of the workspace, like the fully stretched arm, and are avoided by not working at full reach. **Interior singularities** occur inside the workspace when two joint axes line up, as in the wrist of many six-axis arms, and need to be planned around. Gimbal lock in lesson 3.1 was a singularity of exactly this kind.

## Manipulability

How far is a configuration from singular? The Jacobian maps the set of all joint speed vectors of length 1 onto an ellipse of tip velocities, the **manipulability ellipse**. A round ellipse means the tip can move equally easily in every direction. A thin one means some direction is nearly lost. At a singularity it collapses to a line.

A single-number summary is $|\det J|$ for a square Jacobian, which is proportional to the ellipse's area. For the two-link arm that is $L_1L_2|\sin q_2|$, largest when the elbow is at 90°. This is why arms are designed to do their work with the elbow comfortably bent.

=== frc ===

A double-jointed FRC arm is this two-link arm, and its singularities are the reason those mechanisms are hard to drive well. When the arm reaches straight out to score at full extension it is near $q_2 = 0$: the driver asks for a small outward nudge and the joints must swing a long way, slowly, to produce it. Teams deal with this by planning paths in joint space, or by keeping setpoints away from full extension. The force side matters for sizing: $\tau = J^TF$ with $F$ equal to the weight of a game piece at the end of the extended arm gives the holding torque each joint's motor and gearbox must supply, which is how you check a gear ratio before building.

=== summary ===

- $\dot{x} = J(q)\,\dot{q}$, with $J = \partial f/\partial q$. The Jacobian depends on the configuration.
- Column $j$ is the tip velocity per unit speed of joint $j$: $\hat{\omega}_j \times (p_\text{tip} - p_j)$.
- Forces map through the transpose: $\tau = J^T F$.
- Singularity: $J$ loses rank. For the two-link arm, $\det J = L_1L_2\sin q_2$, zero when straight or folded.
- Near a singularity, required joint speeds blow up; along the lost direction the arm resists force with no torque.
- Manipulability measures the distance from singular; keep the elbow bent.
