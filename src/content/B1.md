Everything in this module is written in the language of calculus. You do not need to be able to solve hard integrals for this course. You do need a solid feel for three ideas: the **derivative**, the **integral**, and the **differential equation**. This lesson builds that feel using things you have already seen on a robot.

## The derivative: how fast is it changing?

Suppose an elevator's height $y$ changes over time. Its velocity is how fast the height changes. If you sample the height twice, a short time $\Delta t$ apart, you can estimate it:

$$v \approx \frac{y(t + \Delta t) - y(t)}{\Delta t}$$

That is "change in height divided by change in time", the slope of the height-versus-time graph. The **derivative** is what this ratio becomes as $\Delta t$ shrinks toward zero: the slope at one instant. It is written $\dfrac{dy}{dt}$.

In control and robotics, derivatives with respect to time are so common that there is a shorthand, a dot over the variable:

$$\dot{y} = \frac{dy}{dt} \qquad\qquad \ddot{y} = \frac{d^2y}{dt^2}$$

Two dots means "the derivative of the derivative". The chain you will use constantly is

| Quantity | Symbol | Units |
|---|---|---|
| position | $y$ | m |
| velocity | $\dot{y}$ | m/s |
| acceleration | $\ddot{y}$ | m/s² |

Velocity is the rate of change of position, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Newton's second law, $F = m\ddot{y}$, is a statement about a second derivative.

A few derivatives are worth knowing by heart, because they describe the motions that appear everywhere:

| Function | Its derivative | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| constant $c$ | $0$ | something that does not change has zero rate of change |
| $at$ | $a$ | a straight line has a constant slope |
| $t^2$ | $2t$ | constant acceleration gives a steadily growing speed |
| $\sin(\omega t)$ | $\omega\cos(\omega t)$ | faster oscillation means steeper slopes |
| $e^{at}$ | $a\,e^{at}$ | an exponential's slope is proportional to itself |

The last row is the most important one in this module. Keep it in mind.

## The integral: adding up small changes

The integral runs the same idea backwards. If you know the velocity at every moment, you can recover how far you have travelled by adding up many small steps, each one "velocity × a short time":

$$y(t) \approx y(0) + v_1\,\Delta t + v_2\,\Delta t + v_3\,\Delta t + \cdots$$

As the steps shrink, this sum becomes the **integral**, written $\displaystyle y(t) = y(0) + \int_0^t v(\tau)\,d\tau$. Geometrically it is the area under the velocity-versus-time graph.

So differentiation and integration undo each other: differentiate position to get velocity, integrate velocity to get position back. Note the $y(0)$: integrating only tells you how much something *changed*, so you always need to know where it started.

## Differential equations: a rule for how something changes

Here is the idea that makes the rest of the course work. For most physical systems you cannot write down directly where the thing will be at time $t$. What physics gives you instead is a rule relating a quantity to **its own rate of change**. That rule is a **differential equation**.

A simple example: a spinning wheel with friction. Friction slows the wheel, and the faster it spins, the harder friction pushes back. In symbols, with $\omega$ the wheel's speed:

$$\dot{\omega} = -a\,\omega$$

Read it aloud: "the rate at which speed changes equals minus a constant times the current speed." It does not say what $\omega$ is. It says how $\omega$ moves, given where it is now.

Which function has a slope proportional to itself? From the table above, the exponential. The solution is

$$\omega(t) = \omega(0)\,e^{-at}$$

The speed decays quickly at first, when it is large, then more and more slowly, and approaches zero without ever quite reaching it. This shape, **exponential decay**, shows up in cooling coffee, discharging capacitors and motors coasting to a stop, all for the same reason: the rate of change is proportional to the amount left.

## You can always solve one by stepping

Even when you cannot find a formula, a differential equation tells you exactly how to simulate. Take $\dot{y} = -2y$, starting at $y = 1$, with a step of $\Delta t = 0.1$:

1. The current value is $1$, so the slope is $-2 \times 1 = -2$.
2. Move forward: $y \leftarrow 1 + 0.1 \times (-2) = 0.8$.
3. Now the slope is $-2 \times 0.8 = -1.6$, so $y \leftarrow 0.8 + 0.1 \times (-1.6) = 0.64$.
4. Repeat.

After five steps you get $0.328$. The exact answer at $t = 0.5$ is $e^{-1} = 0.368$. Close, and it gets closer with smaller steps. This is what `solve_ivp` did for you in lesson 0.2, and what a physics engine does thousands of times per second. Lesson 1.3 studies this process in detail.

=== frc ===

You already do both operations in robot code. When WPILib reports an encoder **velocity**, it is differencing positions over time, which is a numerical derivative. That is also why velocity readings are noisier than position readings: dividing a small, slightly noisy difference by a tiny $\Delta t$ magnifies the noise. Going the other way, your gyro measures *turn rate*, and the heading you read from it is that rate **integrated** over time. Small errors in the rate add up, which is why gyro heading slowly drifts during a match.

=== summary ===

- The derivative $\dot{y}$ is the instantaneous rate of change; numerically, $\Delta y / \Delta t$.
- Position → velocity → acceleration is a chain of derivatives; integration goes back up the chain and needs a starting value.
- A differential equation gives the rate of change as a function of the current state.
- $\dot{y} = -a\,y$ has the solution $y(0)\,e^{-at}$: exponential decay.
- Any differential equation can be solved numerically by taking many small steps along the slope.

=== quiz ===

? A robot's position is $y(t) = 3t^2$ metres. What is its velocity at $t = 2$ s?
- 6 m/s
* 12 m/s
- 3 m/s
- 24 m/s
> $\dot{y} = 6t$, so at $t = 2$ the velocity is 12 m/s.

? Your gyro reports a constant turn rate of 10°/s for 3 seconds, starting from a heading of 45°. What is the heading at the end?
- 30°
- 10°
* 75°
- 55°
> Integrate the rate: 10°/s × 3 s = 30° of change, added to the starting value of 45°.

? Which statement describes the differential equation $\dot{y} = -a\,y$ with $a > 0$?
- $y$ decreases at a constant rate until it hits zero
* $y$ decreases quickly when it is large and more slowly as it approaches zero
- $y$ oscillates around zero
- $y$ grows without limit
> The rate of change is proportional to $y$ itself, so the decay slows as $y$ shrinks. That is exponential decay.
