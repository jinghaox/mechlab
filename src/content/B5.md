The next lesson introduces the Jacobian, the matrix that relates joint speeds to hand speeds. It is built from **partial derivatives**: derivatives of functions that depend on more than one variable. If you have met ordinary derivatives (bridge lesson B1), this is a small step, and this lesson takes it.

## Functions of several variables

The tip position of a two-link arm depends on two joint angles:

$$x(q_1, q_2) = L_1\cos q_1 + L_2\cos(q_1 + q_2)$$

One output, two inputs. You cannot ask "what is the slope of $x$?" without saying *in which direction* you are changing the inputs.

A helpful picture: think of a function of two variables as a landscape, where the two inputs are map coordinates and the output is the height of the ground. Standing at one spot, the ground slopes differently depending on which way you face.

## The partial derivative

The **partial derivative** answers the simplest version of the question: how fast does the output change if I vary **one** input and hold all the others fixed?

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \text{the derivative of } f \text{ with respect to } x\text{, treating every other variable as a constant}$$

The curly $\partial$ signals "partial". In the landscape picture, $\partial f/\partial x$ is the slope you feel walking due east, and $\partial f/\partial y$ the slope walking due north.

The mechanics are the ordinary rules of differentiation, with the other variables playing the role of constants. Take

$$f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin y$$

- With respect to $x$, treat $y$ as a constant: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy$. The $\sin y$ term is a constant and vanishes.
- With respect to $y$, treat $x$ as a constant: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + \cos y$.

At the point $(2, 1)$ these are $4$ and $4.54$.

Now the arm. Differentiate the tip's $x$ coordinate with respect to each joint:

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial q_1} = -L_1\sin q_1 - L_2\sin(q_1+q_2) \qquad \frac{\partial x}{\partial q_2} = -L_2\sin(q_1+q_2)$$

Each has a physical meaning: it is how far the tip moves in $x$ **per radian** of that joint, with the other joint locked. Notice that $q_1$ appears in both terms of $x$, so its partial derivative has two terms, while $q_2$ appears in only one.

## Small changes add up

Here is the fact that makes partial derivatives useful. If *both* inputs change by small amounts $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, the output changes by approximately

$$\Delta f \approx \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\Delta y$$

Each input contributes its own slope times its own change, and the contributions simply add. The approximation becomes exact as the changes shrink. This is the multi-variable version of "rise equals slope times run", and it is the same idea as the linearization of lesson 1.6.

For the arm it reads: the change in the tip's $x$ position is (sensitivity to joint 1) × (change in joint 1) + (sensitivity to joint 2) × (change in joint 2).

Divide both sides by a small time $\Delta t$ and the changes become rates. This is the **chain rule**:

$$\dot{f} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\dot{x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\dot{y}$$

So if you know how fast the joints are turning, the partial derivatives tell you how fast the tip is moving.

## The gradient

Collect all the partial derivatives of a function into a vector. That vector is the **gradient**:

$$\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x \\ \partial f/\partial y \end{bmatrix}$$

The "small changes add up" rule is then a dot product, $\Delta f \approx \nabla f \cdot \Delta x$, and that gives the gradient a geometric meaning:

- It **points in the direction of steepest increase**: uphill, on the landscape.
- Its **length** is the slope in that direction.
- Moving perpendicular to it leaves $f$ unchanged: you are walking along a contour line.

To *decrease* a function as fast as possible, step in the direction of $-\nabla f$. Repeating that step is **gradient descent**, the workhorse of numerical optimization and of all machine learning. Lesson 3.7 uses a close relative of it to solve inverse kinematics, and Module 12 uses it to train neural networks.

## From gradient to Jacobian

A robot's tip has more than one coordinate. The planar arm has two outputs, $x$ and $z$, each a function of two inputs, $q_1$ and $q_2$. Each output has its own gradient. Stack them as the **rows** of a matrix:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial q_1} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial q_2} \\[2ex] \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial q_1} & \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial q_2} \end{bmatrix}$$

That matrix is the **Jacobian**. With it, the chain rule for both outputs at once is a single matrix equation:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{z} \end{bmatrix} = J \begin{bmatrix} \dot{q}_1 \\ \dot{q}_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Each **column** of $J$ has its own reading: column $j$ is the velocity of the tip when joint $j$ turns at 1 rad/s and all the other joints are still. You met this matrix once before, without the name: the $A$ matrix of lesson 1.6 was the Jacobian of the dynamics $f(x, u)$ with respect to the state.

## Computing one numerically

When a formula is unavailable or too messy, estimate a partial derivative by doing exactly what the definition says: nudge one input, hold the others, and see how the output responds.

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \approx \frac{f(x + \epsilon,\, y) - f(x - \epsilon,\, y)}{2\epsilon}$$

```python
import numpy as np

def f(x, y):
    return x**2 * y + np.sin(y)

eps = 1e-6
df_dx = (f(2 + eps, 1) - f(2 - eps, 1)) / (2 * eps)   # 4.0
df_dy = (f(2, 1 + eps) - f(2, 1 - eps)) / (2 * eps)   # 4.5403
```

Comparing a hand-derived derivative against this numerical one is the standard way to catch algebra mistakes. Use it every time you derive a Jacobian.

Swerve drive kinematics is a Jacobian you already use. The chassis has three velocities, $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$, and the four modules together have eight velocity components. The 8×3 matrix that maps one to the other is made of partial derivatives: each entry says how much one module's velocity component changes per unit of one chassis velocity. Its columns have the meaning described above. The $\omega$ column is "what every wheel must do for the robot to spin in place at 1 rad/s", and it is the column that depends on where the modules are mounted. `SwerveDriveKinematics` builds that matrix from the module positions you give its constructor.

=== summary ===

- A partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x$ is the rate of change with respect to one variable, holding the others fixed.
- Small changes add: $\Delta f \approx \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Delta y$.
- Chain rule: $\dot{f} = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\dot{x} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\dot{y}$.
- The gradient $\nabla f$ collects the partial derivatives and points in the direction of steepest increase.
- The Jacobian stacks the gradients of several outputs as rows; column $j$ is the effect of input $j$ alone.
- Check any hand-derived derivative with a finite difference.
