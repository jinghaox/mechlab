Poles tell you whether a loop is stable. They do not tell you **how close** it is to being unstable, and they are awkward when the plant includes a time delay or is known only from measurements. The frequency-domain view answers both. It asks one question: *if I feed the system a sine wave, what comes out?* The answer leads to the two most widely used measures of robustness in control, gain margin and phase margin.

## Sine in, sine out

Drive a stable linear system with $u(t) = \sin\omega t$ and wait for the transient to die away. The output is a sine wave at the **same frequency**, with two changes:

- its amplitude is scaled by a factor, the **gain**
- it is shifted in time, by a **phase** angle

Both depend on the frequency $\omega$, and both come straight from the transfer function evaluated at $s = j\omega$:

$$\text{gain} = |G(j\omega)| \qquad\qquad \text{phase} = \angle G(j\omega)$$

This is why complex numbers matter here. $G(j\omega)$ is a complex number; its magnitude is the amplitude ratio and its angle is the phase shift.

For the first-order system $G(s) = \dfrac{1}{\tau s + 1}$:

$$|G(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega\tau)^2}} \qquad \angle G(j\omega) = -\arctan(\omega\tau)$$

Slow sine waves ($\omega \ll 1/\tau$) pass through almost unchanged. Fast ones ($\omega \gg 1/\tau$) are attenuated and delayed by nearly 90°. At $\omega = 1/\tau$, the **corner frequency**, the gain is $1/\sqrt{2} = 0.707$ and the phase is $-45°$. You measured this behaviour in Lab 0.2 when you fed a sine into the RC filter.

## The Bode plot

A **Bode plot** shows gain and phase against frequency on two stacked graphs, with frequency on a logarithmic axis. Gain is plotted in **decibels**:

$$\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}|G|$$

| Gain | dB |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0 dB |
| 0.707 | −3 dB |
| 10 | +20 dB |
| 0.1 | −20 dB |
| 2 | +6 dB |

Decibels turn multiplication into addition, so the Bode plot of two systems in series is the sum of their individual plots. And on these axes common building blocks become straight lines:

| Element | Gain slope | Phase |
|---|---|---|
| constant $K$ | flat | 0° |
| integrator $1/s$ | −20 dB per decade | −90° everywhere |
| real pole $1/(\tau s+1)$ | flat, then −20 dB/decade above $1/\tau$ | 0° falling to −90° |
| real zero $(\tau s+1)$ | flat, then +20 dB/decade above $1/\tau$ | 0° rising to +90° |
| time delay $T$ | flat (gain exactly 1) | $-\omega T$ radians, falling without limit |

A "decade" is a factor of ten in frequency. Each pole eventually contributes −90° of phase; each zero gives +90° back. The time delay is the worst kind of element: it costs phase and provides nothing in return.

## Why a loop goes unstable

Think about a signal travelling around a feedback loop. It passes through the controller and the plant, then is subtracted at the summing junction. The subtraction is what makes feedback corrective.

Now suppose that at some frequency the loop delays the signal by exactly 180°. A sine shifted by 180° is its own negative. Subtracting a negated signal is **adding** it. At that frequency your negative feedback has turned into positive feedback.

If the loop gain at that frequency is less than 1, the signal returns smaller each time around and dies out. If the loop gain is greater than 1, it returns larger each time and grows. So the critical condition is

$$|L(j\omega)| = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \angle L(j\omega) = -180°$$

where $L = CG$ is the loop transfer function. This is the same condition as "a closed-loop pole on the imaginary axis", seen from a different direction.

## Gain margin and phase margin

The two margins measure how far the loop is from that critical condition:

- The **gain margin** is how much the loop gain could be multiplied before the loop goes unstable. It is read at the frequency where the phase is −180°.
- The **phase margin** is how much additional phase lag the loop could absorb before going unstable. It is read at the **crossover frequency** $\omega_c$, where the loop gain is 1 (0 dB).

Common design targets are a phase margin of 45° to 60° and a gain margin of at least 6 dB (a factor of 2).

Phase margin is closely tied to damping. For moderate values, a useful rule is $\zeta \approx \text{PM}/100$ with PM in degrees: 60° of phase margin gives about $\zeta = 0.6$. The lab shows the relationship on the motor loop from lesson 2.4:

| $K_p$ | Gain margin | Phase margin | Crossover | Step overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 26.8 dB | 63° | 2.3 rad/s | 6% |
| 0.1 | 20.8 dB | 47° | 3.9 rad/s | 21% |
| 0.2 | 14.8 dB | 32° | 6.2 rad/s | 39% |
| 0.5 | 6.8 dB | 14° | 10.5 rad/s | 68% |
| 1.0 | 0.8 dB | 2° | 15.1 rad/s | 93% |

Raising the gain pushes the crossover to a higher frequency, where the plant has more phase lag, so the phase margin shrinks. Every row is stable; the margins tell you how fragile each one is. At $K_p = 1.0$ the gain margin of 0.8 dB means a 10% increase in gain, from a warm motor or a fresh battery, would tip it over. The crossover frequency is also a good estimate of the closed loop's **bandwidth**: roughly the fastest reference it can follow.

## Delay: the silent margin killer

A pure time delay $T$ leaves the gain alone and subtracts $\omega T$ radians of phase. At the crossover frequency that is a direct loss of phase margin:

$$\text{phase margin lost} = \omega_c\,T \quad (\text{radians})$$

With $K_p = 0.2$ the crossover is 6.2 rad/s and the phase margin 32°:

| Delay | Phase margin | Loss predicted by $\omega_c T$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 31.7° | |
| 10 ms | 28.1° | 3.6° |
| 50 ms | 13.9° | 17.8° |
| 100 ms | −3.9° | 35.6°, **unstable** |

Two consequences follow. First, any delay in the loop (sensor filtering, computation, communication) spends phase margin, and the faster the loop, the more each millisecond costs. Second, it gives a quick feasibility check: for a decent margin you need $\omega_c T$ well under one radian, so **the total loop delay caps the achievable bandwidth at roughly $1/T$ rad/s or less**.

## Why engineers like this view

- It handles **delays** exactly, which pole analysis cannot.
- It works from **measured data**. You can measure a plant's frequency response by driving it with sine waves, with no model at all.
- It shows the effect of each controller term at a glance. A D term is a zero: it adds phase near crossover, which is why it improves damping. An I term is an integrator: it adds gain at low frequency, which removes steady-state error, and costs phase.

=== frc ===

Every delay on an FRC robot subtracts phase margin: the 20 ms loop period, a CAN status frame that arrives every 10 or 20 ms, and the moving-average filter that a motor controller applies to encoder velocity, which can add tens of milliseconds by itself. Added together they can easily reach 50 ms, and by the rule above that caps a roboRIO-closed velocity loop at a bandwidth of a few rad/s to perhaps 10 rad/s with a healthy margin. This is the reason for running velocity loops **on the motor controller**, at 1 kHz with the raw sensor: the delay drops by more than a factor of ten, and the usable gain rises with it. It is also why reducing the velocity filter window in the motor controller's configuration often makes a previously "untunable" flywheel behave.

=== summary ===

- A sine wave through a linear system comes out at the same frequency, scaled by $|G(j\omega)|$ and shifted by $\angle G(j\omega)$.
- Bode plot: gain in dB and phase against log frequency. Poles add −20 dB/decade and −90°; zeros add the opposite.
- A loop is on the edge of instability when the loop gain is 1 where the phase is −180°.
- Gain margin: allowable gain increase. Phase margin: allowable extra phase lag at crossover. Aim for 45°–60° and ≥ 6 dB.
- $\zeta \approx \text{PM}/100$. A delay $T$ costs $\omega_c T$ radians of phase margin.
