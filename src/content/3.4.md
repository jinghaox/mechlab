**Forward kinematics** answers the first question anyone asks about a robot arm: *given the joint angles, where is the hand?* It is a pure geometry problem with exactly one answer, and it underlies everything else in this module. This lesson works it out for a two-link arm by plain trigonometry, then introduces the two systematic methods used for real robots, Denavit–Hartenberg parameters and the product of exponentials.

## Joint space and task space

A robot is described in two different sets of coordinates:

- **Joint space**: the vector of joint angles $q = (q_1, \dots, q_n)$. This is what the motors control and the encoders measure.
- **Task space**: the pose of the end-effector, its position and orientation. This is what the task cares about.

Forward kinematics is the map from the first to the second:

$$T(q) = \text{pose of the end-effector as a function of the joint angles}$$

## The two-link arm

Take a planar arm with link lengths $L_1$ and $L_2$. The shoulder angle $q_1$ is measured from the horizontal, and the elbow angle $q_2$ is measured **relative to the first link**.

The elbow is at the end of link 1:

$$\text{elbow} = (L_1\cos q_1,\ L_1\sin q_1)$$

Link 2 points in the direction $q_1 + q_2$, since its angle adds to the first link's. So the tip is at

$$x = L_1\cos q_1 + L_2\cos(q_1 + q_2) \qquad z = L_1\sin q_1 + L_2\sin(q_1 + q_2)$$

and the tip's orientation is simply $q_1 + q_2$.

This arm, with $L_1 = 0.30$ m and $L_2 = 0.25$ m, is the one used throughout the labs. At $q = (40°, 65°)$ the tip is at $(0.165, 0.434)$ m.

Trigonometry by inspection works for two or three joints in a plane. For six joints in 3-D you need a method.

## Method 1: a chain of transforms

Lesson 3.3 gave the method already. Attach a frame to each link and multiply the transforms between them. For the planar arm each link contributes "rotate by the joint angle, then move along the link":

$$T(q) = T_1(q_1)\,T_2(q_2), \qquad T_i(q_i) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos q_i & -\sin q_i & L_i\cos q_i \\ \sin q_i & \cos q_i & L_i\sin q_i \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiplying these out reproduces the trigonometric formulas, and the $\cos(q_1+q_2)$ terms appear by themselves from the angle-addition identities. The method extends to any number of joints without further thought.

## Method 2: Denavit–Hartenberg parameters

There is freedom in where to put each link's frame, which makes it hard to compare or tabulate robots. The **Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) convention**, from 1955, fixes the choice, so that every link is described by exactly four numbers:

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\theta$ | rotation about the previous $z$ axis (the joint angle, for a revolute joint) |
| $d$ | offset along the previous $z$ axis |
| $a$ | link length, along the new $x$ axis |
| $\alpha$ | twist about the new $x$ axis, between successive joint axes |

Each joint's axis is its frame's $z$ axis. A robot is then a table with one row per joint, and the forward kinematics is the product of one standard matrix per row. For the two-link arm:

| Joint | $\theta$ | $d$ | $a$ | $\alpha$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $q_1$ | 0 | $L_1$ | 0 |
| 2 | $q_2$ | 0 | $L_2$ | 0 |

The zeros in the $\alpha$ column say the two joint axes are parallel, which is what makes the arm planar.

DH tables are found in most robot datasheets and older textbooks, so you need to be able to read one. Be aware that two incompatible variants exist ("standard" and "modified"), which differ in where the frames sit, and that the frame placement rules are easy to get wrong for unusual geometries.

## Method 3: the product of exponentials

The modern alternative needs no link frames at all. You describe each joint directly **in the base frame**, with the robot in its zero configuration:

- for each joint, the direction $\hat{\omega}_i$ of its axis and any point $r_i$ on that axis,
- the pose $M$ of the end-effector when all joint angles are zero.

Each joint's axis is packed into a 4×4 matrix $[S_i]$ called a **screw axis**:

$$[S_i] = \begin{bmatrix} [\hat{\omega}_i]_\times & -\hat{\omega}_i \times r_i \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

where $[\hat{\omega}]_\times$ is the 3×3 matrix that performs a cross product with $\hat{\omega}$. The forward kinematics is then

$$T(q) = e^{[S_1]q_1}\,e^{[S_2]q_2}\cdots e^{[S_n]q_n}\,M$$

The matrix exponential $e^{[S]q}$ is the transform "rotate by $q$ about that joint axis". This is the same exponential that solved $\dot{x} = Ax$ in Module 1: a joint turning at a steady rate is a linear differential equation for the pose, and this is its solution.

The formula has a direct reading from right to left: start with the hand at its zero pose $M$; turn the last joint, which moves only the hand; then the joint before it, which moves the hand and the last joint together; and so on back to the base.

The advantages are practical. Only two frames need defining, base and tool. There are no placement rules to get wrong. And the joint axes can be read straight from a CAD model or, as in the lab, from MuJoCo: twelve lines of Python extract the screw axes from **any** model, and the resulting forward kinematics matches MuJoCo to $4\times10^{-15}$ on a five-joint arm across 200 random poses.

## What MuJoCo does

MuJoCo computes forward kinematics for you. After `mj_kinematics(model, data)` or `mj_forward`, every body, geom and site has its world pose available:

```python
site = model.site("tip").id
data.site_xpos[site]                    # position, shape (3,)
data.site_xmat[site].reshape(3, 3)      # rotation matrix
```

A **site** is a named marker fixed to a body, and it is the standard way to define a tool point. Knowing the three methods above still matters: you need them to understand what the simulator is doing, to work on real robots that have no simulator in the loop, and because the Jacobian in lesson 3.5 comes from differentiating these expressions.

=== frc ===

An FRC arm on an elevator, or a double-jointed arm, is the two-link arm of this lesson, and the same formulas tell you where the end of the mechanism is. Teams use them for two things. One is the software safety check: computing the end-effector's height and extension from the encoder angles to make sure it stays within the frame-perimeter extension limit and clear of the bumpers. The other is `Mechanism2d`, WPILib's dashboard visualization, which is forward kinematics drawn on screen: you give it link lengths and joint angles and it draws the linkage, with each `MechanismLigament2d` angle specified relative to its parent, as $q_2$ is here.

=== summary ===

- Forward kinematics maps joint angles $q$ to the end-effector pose $T(q)$. It always has exactly one answer.
- Two-link arm: $x = L_1\cos q_1 + L_2\cos(q_1+q_2)$, $z = L_1\sin q_1 + L_2\sin(q_1+q_2)$.
- General method: multiply one transform per joint.
- DH parameters: four numbers $(\theta, d, a, \alpha)$ per joint, with frames placed by convention.
- Product of exponentials: $T(q) = e^{[S_1]q_1}\cdots e^{[S_n]q_n}M$, with joint axes given in the base frame at the zero pose.
- In MuJoCo, read `data.site_xpos` and `data.site_xmat` after `mj_kinematics`.
