Forward kinematics asks where the hand is for given joint angles. Real tasks ask the opposite: *I want the hand **here**; what should the joint angles be?* That is **inverse kinematics** (IK), and it is a much harder question. It may have several answers, or none, and for a general arm there is no formula at all. This lesson covers the cases where a formula does exist, using the two-link arm, and the ideas that carry over to every other arm.

## Why the inverse problem is harder

Forward kinematics is a function: one set of joint angles gives exactly one pose. Its inverse is not a function, and three things can happen.

- **No solution.** The target is outside the **workspace**, the set of points the hand can reach. For the two-link arm the workspace is a ring: no farther from the shoulder than $L_1 + L_2$, and no closer than $|L_1 - L_2|$.
- **Several solutions.** Inside the ring, almost every point can be reached two ways: with the elbow on one side of the line from shoulder to target, or on the other.
- **Infinitely many solutions.** If the arm has more joints than the task needs, it is **redundant**, and a whole family of joint angles gives the same hand pose. Your own arm is like this: hold your hand still on a table and you can still swing your elbow.

The equations themselves are also awkward. They involve sines and cosines of sums of the unknowns, so they are nonlinear, and no general algebraic method solves them.

## The two-link solution

Given a target $(x, z)$, find $q_1$ and $q_2$. Geometry does it in two steps.

**Step 1: the elbow angle.** The shoulder, the elbow and the target form a triangle with sides $L_1$, $L_2$ and $r = \sqrt{x^2 + z^2}$. The elbow angle affects *only* the distance $r$; turning the shoulder does not change the triangle's shape. The law of cosines gives

$$\cos q_2 = \frac{x^2 + z^2 - L_1^2 - L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}$$

You can also get this by squaring and adding the two forward kinematics equations, which makes $q_1$ drop out.

Three cases follow immediately:

- If the right-hand side is larger than 1 or smaller than −1, **there is no solution**. The target is outside the ring.
- Otherwise, $q_2 = \pm\arccos(\cdots)$. The two signs are the two solutions, **elbow-up** and **elbow-down**.
- If the right-hand side is exactly ±1, the two solutions merge into one. That is the workspace boundary, which is also the singularity of lesson 3.5. The two things always coincide: a singularity is where solution branches meet.

**Step 2: the shoulder angle.** The direction to the target is $\operatorname{atan2}(z, x)$. The arm does not point straight at the target; link 2 pulls the tip off the line of link 1 by some angle. Subtract it:

$$q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(z, x) - \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(L_2\sin q_2,\ L_1 + L_2\cos q_2\right)$$

Always use `atan2(y, x)`, never `atan(y/x)`. The two-argument version returns the correct angle in all four quadrants and does not fail when $x = 0$.

For the lab arm ($L_1 = 0.30$, $L_2 = 0.25$) and the target $(0.35, 0.20)$:

| Solution | $q_1$ | $q_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| elbow up | 68.0° | −86.2° |
| elbow down | −8.5° | 86.2° |

Both put the tip on the target to within rounding error, as MuJoCo confirms.

## Choosing between solutions

When several solutions exist, the mathematics has no preference. You choose, on grounds such as these:

- **Continuity.** Pick the solution closest to the current joint angles. Jumping from elbow-up to elbow-down in the middle of a motion would swing the whole arm.
- **Joint limits.** A solution that needs a joint beyond its range is not a solution.
- **Collisions.** One elbow position may hit the table or the robot's own base.
- **Distance from singularities.** Prefer the configuration with better manipulability.

Changing branch means passing through the singularity where the branches meet, with the arm fully stretched, or else making a separate, deliberate move in joint space. A good motion planner decides which branch to use before the motion starts.

## Following a path

A formula for IK makes Cartesian motion easy. To move the tip along a straight line, split the line into many closely spaced points, solve IK for each, and send the resulting joint angles one after another. The lab does this for a line from $(0.20, 0.30)$ to $(0.45, -0.10)$:

- the tip stays on the line to within $10^{-16}$ m,
- while the shoulder sweeps from 17° to 100° and the elbow from −107° to −66°.

A straight line for the hand is a curved, uneven motion for the joints. Equal steps along the path do **not** correspond to equal steps in joint angle, so the joints must speed up and slow down along the way. Near a singularity those joint speeds become extreme, exactly as the Jacobian predicted.

## Beyond two links

Closed-form solutions exist only for arms with special geometry, and industrial arms are designed to have it.

The classic case is the six-axis arm with a **spherical wrist**: its last three joint axes meet at a single point, the wrist centre. That geometry **decouples** the problem. The position of the wrist centre depends only on the first three joints, which is a problem much like the two-link arm with a rotating base. The orientation of the hand is then set by the last three joints, which is the problem of extracting three Euler angles from a rotation matrix. Six unknowns become two sets of three.

Such an arm has up to **eight** solutions for one hand pose: shoulder left or right, elbow up or down, wrist flipped or not. Solvers return all of them and the planner chooses.

When no closed form exists, as for most low-cost and research arms, and for anything redundant, the problem is solved numerically, by iterating with the Jacobian. That is the next lesson. Where a closed form is available it is still preferred: it is thousands of times faster, it finds *every* solution, and it never fails to converge.

=== frc ===

Driving a double-jointed arm to "the high scoring position" is inverse kinematics. Teams with such arms typically define each scoring pose as an $(x, z)$ target for the end-effector and use exactly the two formulas in this lesson to convert it to shoulder and elbow setpoints. The choice of branch is a real design decision: an arm that scores on both sides of the robot uses one elbow configuration for the front and the mirrored one for the back, and the transition between them is planned through a safe tucked pose, not through full extension. Swerve drive has its own, simpler inverse kinematics: `SwerveDriveKinematics.toSwerveModuleStates()` converts a desired chassis velocity into wheel speeds and angles, and because that map is linear it always has exactly one answer.

=== summary ===

- Inverse kinematics: find $q$ for a desired hand pose. It can have zero, several or infinitely many solutions.
- Workspace of the two-link arm: $|L_1 - L_2| \le r \le L_1 + L_2$.
- $\cos q_2 = \dfrac{x^2 + z^2 - L_1^2 - L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}$; the sign of $q_2$ selects elbow-up or elbow-down.
- $q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(z, x) - \operatorname{atan2}(L_2\sin q_2,\ L_1 + L_2\cos q_2)$.
- Choose among solutions by continuity, joint limits, collisions and manipulability.
- A straight line in task space is a curved path in joint space.
- Six-axis arms with a spherical wrist decouple into position and orientation, with up to eight solutions.
