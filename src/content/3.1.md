Modules 1 and 2 dealt with one joint at a time, and one number was enough to say where it was. A robot's hand, a drone or a camera lives in three dimensions, and describing *which way it is facing* turns out to be surprisingly subtle. This module is about geometry: where things are and how they are oriented. It starts with the central object, the rotation matrix.

## Frames

Attach three perpendicular unit arrows, $x$, $y$ and $z$, to a rigid body. That set of axes is a **coordinate frame**. Robotics is largely the bookkeeping of frames: one fixed to the world, one on the robot's base, one on each link, one on the tool, one on the camera.

All frames in this course are **right-handed**: point your right hand's fingers along $x$, curl them toward $y$, and your thumb points along $z$. Positive rotation about an axis follows the same rule: thumb along the axis, fingers curl in the positive direction.

## The rotation matrix

To describe the orientation of a body frame $B$ relative to the world frame $W$, write down where $B$'s three axes point, in world coordinates, and place them side by side as the columns of a matrix:

$$R = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ \hat{x}_B & \hat{y}_B & \hat{z}_B \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix}$$

That is the whole definition. **The columns of a rotation matrix are the body's axes, seen from the world.**

The same matrix does a second job. If a point has coordinates $p_B$ in the body frame, its coordinates in the world frame are

$$p_W = R\,p_B$$

So $R$ both *describes an orientation* and *converts coordinates* from the body frame to the world frame.

The three basic rotations, about the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes by an angle $\theta$, are

$$R_x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & c & -s \\ 0 & s & c \end{bmatrix} \qquad R_y = \begin{bmatrix} c & 0 & s \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -s & 0 & c \end{bmatrix}$$

$$R_z = \begin{bmatrix} c & -s & 0 \\ s & c & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

with $c = \cos\theta$ and $s = \sin\theta$. $R_z$ is the 2-D rotation from bridge lesson B3 with a third axis left alone.

## Two properties that make them special

Because the columns are perpendicular unit vectors, a rotation matrix satisfies

$$R^T R = I \qquad\text{and}\qquad \det R = +1$$

The first has a very practical consequence: **the inverse of a rotation is its transpose**, $R^{-1} = R^T$. Undoing a rotation costs nothing to compute. The second says a rotation preserves volume and does not mirror.

These are six constraints on nine numbers, which leaves three free. Orientation in 3-D has **three degrees of freedom**, and a rotation matrix spends nine numbers to store them. It is redundant, but it is the one representation that has no special cases, and it is what you should compute with.

## Combining rotations: order matters

Rotations compose by matrix multiplication, and as with all matrices, the order matters. Turn a book 90° about a vertical axis and then 90° about a horizontal one. Now do the two turns in the other order. The book ends up facing differently. In the lab:

$$R_z(90°)R_x(90°)\,\hat{x} = \hat{y} \qquad\qquad R_x(90°)R_z(90°)\,\hat{x} = \hat{z}$$

There is a rule for which side to multiply on, and it is worth memorizing:

- Multiply on the **right** to rotate about an axis of the **body's own** current frame.
- Multiply on the **left** to rotate about an axis of the **fixed world** frame.

A robot arm's joints each rotate relative to the link before them, so a chain of joints is a product read left to right: $R = R_1 R_2 R_3 \cdots$.

## Euler angles

Nine numbers are awkward for a human. The familiar alternative is three angles applied in sequence. The convention used in aerospace and in most of robotics is **yaw, pitch, roll**:

1. **yaw** $\psi$ about the $z$ axis,
2. then **pitch** $\theta$ about the *new* $y$ axis,
3. then **roll** $\phi$ about the *new* $x$ axis.

Each rotation is about the body's own axis, so by the rule above the matrices multiply on the right:

$$R = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\phi)$$

Euler angles are easy to read and use the minimum three numbers. They also come with two traps.

**Trap 1: there are many conventions.** Twelve axis sequences exist, each in a body-axis and a fixed-axis version. "Roll, pitch, yaw" means nothing until you know the sequence. Always check the documentation of whatever library or sensor gives you angles.

**Trap 2: gimbal lock.** Pitch the body up by exactly 90°. Its $x$ axis now points along the world $z$ axis, so the roll axis and the yaw axis are the *same line*. Yawing and rolling do the same thing. In the lab, at 90° pitch:

- (yaw 30°, roll 0°) and (yaw 0°, roll −30°) give the identical matrix,
- so do (yaw 30°, roll 0°) and (yaw 50°, roll 20°).

Only the difference yaw − roll matters there. One of the three degrees of freedom has vanished from the representation, though the body itself can still turn any way it likes.

It is not just a problem at exactly 90°. Near it, tiny physical rotations need huge changes in the angles. The lab turns the body by 1° about the world $x$ axis and reports how much the Euler angles change:

| Pitch | Change in yaw | Change in roll |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | 0.00° | 1.00° |
| 60° | 1.73° | 2.00° |
| 85° | 11.3° | 11.3° |
| 89° | 45.0° | 45.0° |

A controller that works in Euler angles, or an estimator that integrates them, will misbehave badly in that region. Every set of three angles has such a singularity somewhere. The practical policy: **store and compute with rotation matrices or quaternions (next lesson); convert to Euler angles only to show a human.**

=== frc ===

On an FRC robot the yaw from the gyro is the one Euler angle you use constantly, and because the robot stays nearly flat on the floor, pitch stays far from 90° and gimbal lock never bites. The 3-D classes are still built to be safe: WPILib's `Rotation3d` stores a quaternion internally, and its `getX()`, `getY()`, `getZ()` (roll, pitch, yaw) are conversions for display. You do meet the frame bookkeeping of this lesson directly when placing a camera for vision: the camera's mounting orientation is a `Rotation3d`, and getting the sign or the order of its roll, pitch and yaw wrong is one of the most common causes of pose estimates that are mirrored or tilted.

=== summary ===

- A frame is three perpendicular unit axes fixed to a body. Use right-handed frames.
- The columns of $R$ are the body's axes expressed in the world frame; $p_W = R\,p_B$.
- $R^T R = I$ and $\det R = 1$, so $R^{-1} = R^T$. Orientation has three degrees of freedom.
- Rotations do not commute. Multiply on the right for body-axis rotations, on the left for world-axis rotations.
- Yaw–pitch–roll: $R = R_z(\psi)R_y(\theta)R_x(\phi)$. At ±90° pitch, yaw and roll coincide: gimbal lock.
- Compute with matrices or quaternions; show Euler angles to people.
