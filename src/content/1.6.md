Everything since lesson 1.1 has assumed a **linear** system. Real robots are not linear: gravity on an arm goes with the sine of its angle, drag goes with the square of speed, motors saturate. Yet linear tools are used to control almost all of them. The bridge between the two is **linearization**: near an operating point, a smooth nonlinear system behaves like a linear one. This lesson shows how to find that linear model and, just as important, how far to trust it.

## A nonlinear system

A pendulum of length $L$ and mass $m$, with angle $\theta$ measured from hanging straight down, a little damping $d$ and a motor torque $u$ at the pivot, obeys

$$\ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta - d\,\dot{\theta} + \frac{1}{mL^2}u$$

With state $x = \begin{bmatrix}\theta \\ \omega\end{bmatrix}$ this is $\dot{x} = f(x, u)$:

$$f(x, u) = \begin{bmatrix}\omega \\ -\dfrac{g}{L}\sin\theta - d\,\omega + \dfrac{1}{mL^2}u\end{bmatrix}$$

The $\sin\theta$ is what makes it nonlinear. You cannot write this as $Ax + Bu$, because no constant matrix multiplied by $\theta$ gives $\sin\theta$. Superposition fails too: releasing the pendulum from 60° does not give twice the motion of releasing it from 30°. You saw that in Lab 0.3, where the period grew with amplitude.

## Step 1: find an equilibrium

An **equilibrium** is a state where the system stays put if you leave it there: a pair $(x_0, u_0)$ with

$$f(x_0, u_0) = 0$$

For the pendulum with no torque, that requires $\omega = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 0$. There are two solutions:

- $\theta = 0$: hanging straight down.
- $\theta = \pi$: balanced straight up.

Both are genuine equilibria. Everyday experience says they are very different, and the mathematics is about to agree.

## Step 2: approximate near that point

Zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line. Near $\theta = 0$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$: the famous small-angle approximation. Near $\theta = \pi$, writing $\theta = \pi + \delta$, we get $\sin(\pi + \delta) = -\sin\delta \approx -\delta$. The sign has flipped.

In general, define the **deviations** from the equilibrium, $\delta x = x - x_0$ and $\delta u = u - u_0$. To first order,

$$\dot{\delta x} = A\,\delta x + B\,\delta u, \qquad A = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x_0, u_0} \qquad B = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\bigg|_{x_0, u_0}$$

$A$ and $B$ are **Jacobian matrices**. The entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A$ answers: "if I nudge state $j$ a little, how much does the rate of change of state $i$ respond?" Each entry is a slope, evaluated at the equilibrium and then frozen as a constant.

For the pendulum, differentiate each row of $f$ with respect to $\theta$ and $\omega$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\dfrac{g}{L}\cos\theta_0 & -d \end{bmatrix} \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \dfrac{1}{mL^2} \end{bmatrix}$$

The only place the operating point enters is $\cos\theta_0$, which is $+1$ hanging down and $-1$ balanced upright.

## Step 3: read the result

With $L = 0.5$ m, $g/L = 19.62$. Take $d = 0.1$.

| | Hanging down | Balanced upright |
|---|---|---|
| $A$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -19.62 & -0.1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ +19.62 & -0.1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Eigenvalues | $-0.05 \pm 4.43j$ | $+4.38$ and $-4.48$ |
| Behaviour | stable, lightly damped oscillation | **unstable** |

One sign change tells the whole story. Hanging down, the eigenvalues are a complex pair in the left half-plane: nudge the pendulum and it swings at $\sqrt{g/L} = 4.43$ rad/s and slowly settles. Upright, one eigenvalue is positive: the smallest disturbance grows like $e^{4.38t}$, doubling roughly every 0.16 s. That number tells you how fast a balancing controller has to be.

This is the general pattern of use: **the eigenvalues of the linearized $A$ matrix tell you whether an equilibrium of the nonlinear system is stable.** And the linear model $(A, B)$ at the upright position is exactly what you will design a balancing controller on in Modules 2 and 8.

## Remember the deviation variables

The linear model is written in $\delta x$ and $\delta u$, not $x$ and $u$. This is the most common source of mistakes. Consider an arm held horizontally, which needs a constant torque $u_0$ to resist gravity. The linear model's input is the torque *in addition to* $u_0$. A controller designed on the linear model outputs $\delta u$; what you send to the motor is $u_0 + \delta u$. That constant $u_0$ is a feedforward term.

## How far can you trust it?

Linearization is exact only at the equilibrium and degrades as you move away. How fast depends on how curved the nonlinearity is. For the pendulum, the lab measures the worst-case error of the linear model over 5 s:

| Released from | Error of the linear model |
|---|---|
| 5° | 0.03° (1%) |
| 30° | 6.7° (22%) |
| 90° | 133° (useless) |

The error at 30° is mostly a timing error: the real pendulum's period is slightly longer, so the two motions drift out of step. A feedback controller tolerates this well, because it keeps correcting. But no controller designed at one operating point can be assumed to work far from it.

When the operating range is wide, engineers linearize at several points and switch or blend between the resulting controllers (*gain scheduling*), or use methods that handle the nonlinearity directly. Module 4 does the latter for robot arms.

## When you cannot differentiate by hand

For a six-joint arm $f$ is far too complicated to differentiate on paper. Compute the Jacobians numerically instead: nudge each state slightly, see how $f$ changes, and divide.

$$A_{:,j} \approx \frac{f(x_0 + \epsilon e_j,\ u_0) - f(x_0 - \epsilon e_j,\ u_0)}{2\epsilon}$$

Here $e_j$ is a vector with a 1 in position $j$ and zeros elsewhere. The lab implements this in a dozen lines, and MuJoCo provides the same thing for whole robots as `mujoco.mjd_transitionFD`.

=== frc ===

An FRC arm is this pendulum with a motor on it, measured from horizontal rather than from hanging down. Gravity's torque on it is proportional to $\cos\theta$, and that is the arm feedforward term $k_G\cos\theta$: the equilibrium torque $u_0$ needed to hold each angle. Your PID then only handles the *deviation* from that equilibrium, which is why an arm with a proper $k_G$ term is so much easier to tune. It also explains why gains tuned with the arm horizontal can feel different near vertical: the linearized dynamics change with the operating point.

=== summary ===

- Nonlinear system: $\dot{x} = f(x, u)$. Equilibrium: $f(x_0, u_0) = 0$.
- Linearize: $\dot{\delta x} = A\,\delta x + B\,\delta u$ with $A = \partial f/\partial x$ and $B = \partial f/\partial u$ at the equilibrium.
- The eigenvalues of $A$ decide whether the equilibrium is stable.
- Pendulum: hanging down gives $\pm j\sqrt{g/L}$ (stable); upright gives $\pm\sqrt{g/L}$ (unstable).
- The model is in deviation variables and is only valid near the operating point.
- Jacobians can always be computed numerically by finite differences.
