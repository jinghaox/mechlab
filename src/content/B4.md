Lesson 1.5 claimed that the poles of a system are the **eigenvalues** of its $A$ matrix, and asked you to take that on trust. This lesson explains what eigenvalues are and why they decide whether a system is stable. The idea is one of the most useful in all of engineering mathematics, and it rests on a simple picture.

## A direction the matrix does not turn

A matrix transforms vectors: it generally stretches them **and** changes their direction. But for most matrices there are a few special directions that the matrix only stretches, without turning. A vector $v$ along such a direction satisfies

$$Av = \lambda v$$

The vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$, and the number $\lambda$ (lambda), the factor it is stretched by, is the matching **eigenvalue**. "Eigen" is German for "own" or "characteristic": these are the matrix's own directions.

Take

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Try the vector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} = 3\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

Same direction, three times as long. So $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3. Now try $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$: the result is $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ itself, so it is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. Any other vector, say $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$, comes out pointing somewhere new.

Only the direction matters. Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, with the same eigenvalue.

## Finding them

Rearranging $Av = \lambda v$ gives $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$. For a non-zero $v$ to be sent to zero, the matrix $A - \lambda I$ must be singular, so its determinant must vanish:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

This is the **characteristic equation**. For a 2×2 matrix $\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$ it works out to a quadratic:

$$\lambda^2 - (a + d)\,\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0$$

The coefficient $a + d$ is the **trace** (the sum of the diagonal) and $ad - bc$ is the determinant. For the example, $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0$, which factors as $(\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0$. The eigenvalues are 3 and 1, as found above.

An $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues. For anything larger than 2×2, let the computer find them.

## Why they govern dynamics

Here is the connection to control. Consider the system $\dot{x} = Ax$, and suppose the state starts exactly along an eigenvector, $x(0) = v$. Then

$$\dot{x} = Av = \lambda v$$

The rate of change points along $v$ too. So the state never leaves that line; it only moves in and out along it, and along that line the vector equation collapses to the scalar one, $\dot{x} = \lambda x$. You know its solution:

$$x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\,v$$

**Along an eigenvector, a complicated coupled system behaves like a single first-order system with rate $\lambda$.**

Any starting state can be written as a combination of eigenvectors, and because the system is linear, each piece evolves independently:

$$x(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}v_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}v_2 + \cdots$$

Every response of the system is a mixture of the terms $e^{\lambda_i t}$. These terms are called the **modes** of the system. That is why the eigenvalues are the poles: they are the exponents that appear in every response.

## An example

Take a mass-spring-damper with $m = 1$, $k = 2$, $c = 3$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$$

The trace is $-3$ and the determinant is $2$, so $\lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = 0$, giving $\lambda = -1$ and $\lambda = -2$. The eigenvectors are $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}$.

Notice that the characteristic equation is $s^2 + 3s + 2 = 0$ with $\lambda$ in place of $s$, which is the denominator of this system's transfer function. The poles and the eigenvalues are the same numbers because they are roots of the same polynomial.

The eigenvectors have a physical reading here. Each is a (position, velocity) pair. Start the system with velocity equal to minus its position and it glides to rest as $e^{-t}$, with position and velocity shrinking in proportion. Start it with velocity equal to minus twice its position and it decays as $e^{-2t}$. Any other start is a blend of these two modes.

## Complex eigenvalues

A rotation matrix turns *every* vector, so it has no real direction that stays put. Its characteristic equation has complex roots. That is the general rule: **complex eigenvalues mean the matrix involves rotation**, and in a dynamic system rotation in the state plane means **oscillation**.

From bridge lesson B2, $e^{(\sigma + j\omega)t}$ oscillates at $\omega$ with an envelope $e^{\sigma t}$. So for the eigenvalues of $A$:

| Eigenvalue | Mode |
|---|---|
| real and negative | decays exponentially |
| real and positive | grows exponentially |
| complex with negative real part | decaying oscillation |
| complex with positive real part | growing oscillation |

The stability rule follows immediately: $\dot{x} = Ax$ is stable exactly when **every eigenvalue has a negative real part**, because then every mode decays.

## In Python

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0, 1],
              [-2, -3]])

values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)
values             # [-1., -2.]
vectors            # columns are the eigenvectors, scaled to length 1
np.linalg.eigvals(A)   # eigenvalues only
```

The eigenvectors come back as the **columns** of the second result, normalized to unit length, so $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ appears as $\begin{bmatrix}0.707 \\ -0.707\end{bmatrix}$.

=== summary ===

- $Av = \lambda v$: an eigenvector keeps its direction; the eigenvalue is its stretch factor.
- Eigenvalues solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. For 2×2: $\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det = 0$.
- For $\dot{x} = Ax$, each eigenvector direction evolves as $e^{\lambda t}$: one mode per eigenvalue.
- Eigenvalues of $A$ are the poles of the system.
- Complex eigenvalues mean oscillation. Stable ⇔ all real parts negative.
