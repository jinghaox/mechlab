Mechatronics is what you get when mechanics, electronics and software are designed **together** so that a machine can sense what is happening and act on it. A robot arm, a drone, a car's cruise control and a 3D printer are all mechatronic systems. Robotics is the part of the field that deals with machines that move through, or manipulate, the physical world.

This first lesson gives you the one picture that the entire course hangs on.

- The **plant** is the physical thing you want to control: an arm joint, a wheel, a heater. It has dynamics, which means it does not respond instantly.
- The **sensor** measures what the plant is actually doing: an encoder for angle, an IMU for rotation rate.
- The **controller** compares what you want with what you measured and decides what to do. Today it is almost always software.
- The **actuator** turns that decision into physical effort: a motor, a valve, a heating element.

The signals have standard names that you will see in every textbook and in every later lesson:

| Symbol | Name | Example: one arm joint |
|---|---|---|
| $r$ | reference (setpoint) | the angle you want, 90° |
| $y$ | output | the angle the joint is really at |
| $e = r - y$ | error | how far off you are |
| $u$ | control input | the voltage or torque command |
| $d$ | disturbance | gravity, friction, someone bumping the arm |

The loop runs continuously. In a digital controller it runs at a fixed rate, typically 50 Hz to several kHz: read the sensor, compute $e$, compute $u$, send it to the actuator, wait for the next tick.

## Why bother closing the loop?

You could skip the sensor and simply command "apply 6 V for 0.8 seconds". That is **open-loop** control. It works only as long as nothing changes: the same battery voltage, the same load, the same friction. Closing the loop lets the system correct for things you did not predict or model, because any effect that moves $y$ away from $r$ shows up in $e$ and gets pushed back.

Feedback is not free. A loop reacts to errors after they happen, sensors add noise, and a badly tuned loop can oscillate or go unstable. Most of Module 2 is about getting the benefits without those costs.

## Three ways the real world differs from the diagram

1. **The plant has dynamics.** A motor does not reach speed instantly, and an arm keeps moving after you stop pushing. Module 1 teaches you to describe this with differential equations.
2. **Everything is limited.** Motors saturate, batteries sag, sensors have finite resolution. A controller designed on paper for unlimited torque will misbehave on hardware.
3. **Time is sampled.** The controller sees the world in snapshots and holds its output between them. A loop that runs too slowly for its plant is unstable no matter how it is tuned.

=== frc ===

You have already built this loop. On an FRC elevator the **plant** is the carriage and its load, the **actuator** is the motor and its controller, the **sensor** is the encoder, and the **controller** is the `PIDController` running in your 20 ms robot loop. The setpoint you pass in is $r$, the encoder reading is $y$, and the value you send to the motor is $u$. Gravity pulling the carriage down is a disturbance $d$, which is exactly why elevators need a gravity feedforward term.

=== summary ===

- A mechatronic system is a loop: **controller → actuator → plant → sensor → controller**.
- Error is $e = r - y$. The controller turns $e$ into the control input $u$.
- Open loop is simple but cannot correct for disturbances or modeling errors; closed loop can.
- Real loops are limited by plant dynamics, actuator saturation, sensor noise and sample rate.
