A first-order system can only approach its target smoothly. Real mechanisms do something more interesting: they **overshoot** and **oscillate**. An arm that bounces after a move and a car that rocks after a bump are second-order systems. This lesson gives you the two numbers that describe every such response.

## The mass-spring-damper

A mass $m$ is attached to a wall by a spring of stiffness $k$ and a damper with coefficient $c$. A force $F$ pushes on it, and $x$ is its position measured from rest.

- The spring pulls back in proportion to displacement: $-kx$.
- The damper resists in proportion to velocity: $-c\dot{x}$.

Newton's second law, $m\ddot{x} = \text{sum of forces}$, gives

$$m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = F$$

This is second-order because it contains a second derivative. Physically, it has **two places to store energy**: the mass stores kinetic energy and the spring stores potential energy. Energy sloshing back and forth between the two is what makes oscillation possible. A first-order system has only one store, so it cannot oscillate.

## The standard form

Divide by $m$ and rename the coefficients:

$$\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_n\,\dot{x} + \omega_n^2\,x = \omega_n^2\,u$$

where $u = F/k$ is the position the force would eventually hold the mass at. The two new parameters are

$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \qquad\qquad \zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}$$

- **$\omega_n$, the natural frequency** (rad/s), is how fast the system would oscillate with no damping at all. A stiffer spring raises it; more mass lowers it. It sets the *time scale* of the response.
- **$\zeta$ (zeta), the damping ratio**, has no units. It compares the actual damping to the amount that would just barely prevent oscillation. It sets the *shape* of the response.

Any second-order system, mechanical or electrical, can be put in this form. A series RLC circuit, for instance, has $\omega_n = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and $\zeta = \tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$: inductance plays the role of mass, and resistance the role of the damper.

## Four kinds of response

The damping ratio alone decides which of four behaviours you get after a step input:

| Damping ratio | Name | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| $\zeta = 0$ | undamped | oscillates forever at $\omega_n$ |
| $0 < \zeta < 1$ | underdamped | overshoots, then rings with shrinking amplitude |
| $\zeta = 1$ | critically damped | the fastest approach with no overshoot |
| $\zeta > 1$ | overdamped | no overshoot, but sluggish |

## The underdamped case in numbers

For $0 < \zeta < 1$ the step response is a decaying sinusoid:

$$x(t) = 1 - \frac{e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\,\sin\!\left(\omega_d t + \phi\right), \qquad \phi = \arccos\zeta$$

You do not need to memorize this, but two pieces of it matter a great deal:

- The envelope $e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}$ decays with time constant $1/(\zeta\omega_n)$. The product $\zeta\omega_n$ is the **decay rate**.
- The oscillation runs at the **damped frequency** $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, slightly lower than $\omega_n$.

From these come the three specifications people actually quote:

| Quantity | Formula | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Peak time | $t_p = \dfrac{\pi}{\omega_d}$ | both |
| Overshoot | $M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ | $\zeta$ only |
| Settling time (2%) | $t_s \approx \dfrac{4}{\zeta\omega_n}$ | the decay rate |

Overshoot depends on the damping ratio and nothing else, which makes $\zeta$ easy to read from a plot:

| $\zeta$ | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.4 | 0.5 | 0.7 | 1.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overshoot | 73% | 53% | 25% | 16% | 4.6% | 0% |

A value near $\zeta = 0.7$ is a common design target: fast, with an overshoot of only a few percent. Many well-tuned control loops end up there.

## Why this matters for control

In Module 2 you will wrap a controller around a motor, and the closed loop will behave like a second-order system whose $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$ **you** choose through the controller gains. Raising the proportional gain acts like a stiffer spring: faster, but more oscillatory. Adding derivative gain acts like a damper. Tuning a PID loop is largely the art of placing $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$ where you want them.

=== frc ===

Run an arm or elevator with only a P term and it oscillates around the setpoint: the P gain is a virtual **spring** pulling toward the target ($u = k_P \cdot \text{error}$), and the mechanism's inertia is the mass. That is an underdamped mass-spring system with very little damping. Adding a D term adds a virtual **damper** ($k_D \cdot$ rate of error), which raises $\zeta$ and kills the ringing. "Raise P until it oscillates, then add D until it stops" is this lesson in the form of a tuning recipe.

=== summary ===

- $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = F$ in standard form is $\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = \omega_n^2 u$.
- $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ sets the time scale; $\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})$ sets the shape.
- $\zeta < 1$ overshoots and rings; $\zeta = 1$ is the fastest response without overshoot; $\zeta > 1$ is sluggish.
- Overshoot $= e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$; settling time $\approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n)$; damped frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$.
- $\zeta \approx 0.7$ is a good default target.
