The PID controller is three simple ideas added together. It runs most of the world's control loops, from thermostats to industrial robots, and it is what your FRC mechanisms already use. This lesson looks at each term separately on a real control problem: holding a robot arm horizontal against gravity.

## The controller

With error $e = r - y$, the PID control law is

$$u = \underbrace{K_p\,e}_{\text{present}} + \underbrace{K_i\int_0^t e\,d\tau}_{\text{past}} + \underbrace{K_d\,\dot{e}}_{\text{future}}$$

- **P** reacts to the error **now**.
- **I** reacts to the error accumulated over the **past**.
- **D** reacts to where the error is **heading**.

## The test problem

The arm from this module's labs is a 0.4 m link with a load at the end and a motor at the shoulder. It has inertia $I = 0.108$ kg·m² about the joint and a little friction. Gravity pulls on it with a torque of $2.94\,\sin\theta$ N·m, where $\theta = 0$ is hanging straight down. So at 90°, the arm needs a constant 2.94 N·m just to stay where it is.

The task: start hanging down, move to 90° and hold. The results below are from the lab.

## P: a spring

$u = K_p e$ pulls toward the target with a force proportional to the distance from it. That is exactly what a spring does, and the arm responds like a mass on a spring: it overshoots and rings. With $K_p = 20$ N·m/rad:

- **It oscillates.** The equivalent natural frequency is $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_p/I} = 13.6$ rad/s, and the only damping is the joint's own friction, giving $\zeta \approx 0.07$. The arm overshoots and swings for seconds.
- **It stops in the wrong place.** The arm settles at 81.7°, an error of 8.3°.

The error is not a tuning mistake. At rest, the motor torque must balance gravity: $K_p e = 2.94\sin\theta$, so $e \approx 2.94/20 = 0.147$ rad $= 8.4°$. A P controller produces torque only when there is error, and holding the arm up requires torque. Raising $K_p$ shrinks the error but makes the ringing faster and worse.

## D: a damper

$u = K_d\dot{e}$ opposes motion: when the error is shrinking quickly, it pulls back, like a hand slowing the arm before it arrives. It behaves as a damper, adding directly to the system's damping:

$$\zeta = \frac{b + K_d}{2\sqrt{K_p I}}$$

With $K_d = 2$, the damping ratio goes from 0.07 to 0.75. The overshoot disappears and the ringing is gone.

But D does nothing about the final error: once the arm stops, $\dot{e} = 0$ and the D term is silent. The PD-controlled arm stops cleanly at 81.7°, still 8.3° short.

## I: a memory

$u = K_i\int e\,dt$ adds up the error over time. As long as the arm sits below its target, the integral keeps growing, and the torque keeps rising, until the error is gone. At that point the integral stops changing and simply holds whatever value was needed. Here it ends up holding exactly 2.94 N·m. **The integral term finds the gravity torque by itself**, without knowing anything about the arm.

That is its great strength: it removes steady-state error caused by *any* constant disturbance or modelling error.

It has a price. The integral responds to the past, so it always acts late. In the lab, adding $K_i = 40$ to the P controller, without D, gives a response that never settles: the arm keeps swinging around 90°. The lag from the integrator has eaten the small amount of damping the arm had. Lesson 2.4 shows how to predict this exactly.

## All three together

| Controller | Overshoot | Settles? | Final error |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 37% | slowly, after long ringing | 8.3° |
| PD | 0% | yes, cleanly | 8.3° |
| PI | 38% | keeps oscillating | about 0° on average |
| PID | 4% | in 0.59 s | 0° |

Each term fixes what the others cannot: P provides the basic pull, D supplies damping, and I removes the leftover error. The damping from D is also what makes it safe to add I.

## In code

A digital controller runs at a fixed period $\Delta t$ and replaces the integral with a running sum and the derivative with a difference:

```python
error = r - y
integral += error * dt
derivative = (error - previous_error) / dt
previous_error = error
u = kp * error + ki * integral + kd * derivative
```

These five lines are the heart of every PID implementation. The `PID` class used in the labs adds a few practical refinements (output limits, a derivative filter, protection against integrator windup) that lesson 2.6 explains.

One thing to get right from the start: the gains have **units**. Here $K_p$ is in N·m per radian, $K_i$ in N·m per radian-second, and $K_d$ in N·m per (rad/s). Change the units of your measurement from radians to degrees, or of your output from torque to volts, and every gain changes. Most "these gains worked on the other robot" problems are unit problems.

=== frc ===

WPILib's `PIDController` is the five lines above. Its $k_P$, $k_I$, $k_D$ are these same three gains, and `calculate(measurement, setpoint)` runs once per 20 ms loop. Two details from this lesson show up directly in FRC practice. First, teams usually tune arms and elevators with P and D only, and then cancel gravity with a feedforward ($k_G$) rather than with $k_I$, because feedforward acts instantly and the integral acts late. Second, the units point: gains tuned with the encoder in rotations are different by a factor of $2\pi$ from gains tuned in radians, and by a gear ratio from gains tuned at the motor shaft.

=== summary ===

- $u = K_p e + K_i\int e\,dt + K_d\dot{e}$.
- **P** acts like a spring: faster with more gain, but oscillatory, and it leaves a steady-state error against constant loads.
- **D** acts like a damper: it removes overshoot but cannot fix steady-state error and (as lesson 2.6 shows) amplifies noise.
- **I** removes steady-state error by accumulating it, but its lag reduces stability.
- Gains have units. Changing measurement or output units changes the gains.
