Module 1 was about describing systems. This module is about making them do what you want. The central idea is the one from lesson 0.1, **feedback**: measure the output, compare it with the target, and act on the difference. This lesson shows, with numbers, exactly what feedback buys you and what it costs.

## Open loop: computing the answer in advance

Take the motor from Lab 1.1. Its speed obeys $\tau\dot{\omega} + \omega = K u$ with gain $K = 50$ rad/s per volt. You want 100 rad/s. The obvious approach is to invert the model: apply

$$u = \frac{r}{K} = \frac{100}{50} = 2\ \text{V}$$

This is **open-loop** control. No sensor is involved. On the exact motor you modelled, it works perfectly. The trouble starts when reality differs from the model:

- **The plant changes.** Suppose friction is 50% higher than you assumed, because the gearbox is cold or worn. The real gain drops to 33.3, and 2 V gives 66.7 rad/s. A 50% error in a parameter became a 33% error in the output.
- **A disturbance appears.** A load torque that the model never mentioned slows the motor, and nothing responds.

Open-loop control is only as good as your model, and it cannot see anything the model left out.

## Closing the loop

Now add a speed sensor and let the controller act on the error $e = r - \omega$. The simplest rule is **proportional control**:

$$u = K_p\,e$$

The further you are from the target, the harder you push. Substitute into the plant equation and solve for steady state ($\dot{\omega} = 0$):

$$\omega = K K_p (r - \omega) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \frac{K K_p}{1 + K K_p}\,r$$

The product $L = K K_p$ is the **loop gain**: the total amplification once around the loop. It is the single most important quantity in feedback design. With $K_p = 0.2$, $L = 10$ and the motor settles at $\tfrac{10}{11} \times 100 = 90.9$ rad/s.

That is not 100. A proportional controller needs a non-zero error to produce any output, so it always leaves a **steady-state error** of $\dfrac{1}{1+L}$. Hold that thought.

## What feedback buys

Run the same three tests on both controllers. These numbers come from the lab:

| | Nominal plant | Friction +50% | With load torque |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open loop | 100.0 | 66.7 | 80.0 |
| P control, $L = 10$ | 90.9 | 87.0 | 89.1 |
| PI control | 100.0 | 100.0 | 100.0 |

**Robustness to plant changes.** With friction up 50%, open loop lost 33 rad/s. The closed loop lost 4. The loop gain fell from 10 to 6.7, but $\tfrac{L}{1+L}$ barely moves when $L$ is large: it went from 0.909 to 0.870.

**Disturbance rejection.** The load torque cost the open-loop system 20 rad/s. In closed loop it cost 1.8, smaller by the factor $1 + L = 11$. The loop notices the speed dropping and pushes back.

**Speed of response.** Feedback also makes the system faster. The closed-loop time constant is

$$\tau_{cl} = \frac{\tau}{1 + L}$$

so the 0.2 s motor now responds in 18 ms. It gets there by briefly applying far more than 2 V at the start, which is something the open-loop controller never does.

All three benefits scale with $1 + L$. This is the core promise of feedback: **high loop gain makes the output depend on the sensor and the reference, and very little on the plant.**

## What feedback costs

If more loop gain is always better, why not make $K_p$ enormous? Three things stop you, and the rest of this module is about them.

1. **Stability.** Real plants have delays and extra dynamics. Push the gain high enough and the corrections arrive too late, overshoot, and grow. The loop oscillates or diverges. A first-order plant cannot do this, which is why this lesson's example behaves so well; lesson 2.4 shows where it goes wrong.
2. **Actuator limits.** That 18 ms response needs a large initial voltage. Real drivers saturate.
3. **Sensor noise.** The loop cannot tell noise from real motion. High gain amplifies noise straight into the actuator. And any *error* in the sensor is followed faithfully: feedback makes the output as accurate as the measurement, no better.

## Removing the leftover error

The third row of the table shows the fix for steady-state error: add a term that accumulates the error over time.

$$u = K_p\,e + K_i\int e\,dt$$

As long as any error remains, the integral keeps growing and pushes harder, so the only possible resting state is $e = 0$. The result is exactly 100 rad/s in every test, regardless of friction or load. This is **PI control**, and the next lesson examines each term properly.

=== frc ===

Setting a flywheel with `motor.set(0.7)` is open-loop control: 70% of battery voltage, whatever that happens to be. As the battery sags during a match the wheel slows down, and when a game piece passes through, the speed dips and stays down for a while. Switching to a velocity PID closes the loop, and both problems shrink by the factor $1 + L$. The best shooters combine the two: a feedforward term $k_V \cdot v$ supplies the open-loop answer, and feedback corrects whatever the model missed. Lesson 2.6 covers that combination.

=== summary ===

- Open loop: $u = r/K$. Exact only if the model is exact and nothing disturbs the plant.
- Closed loop with $u = K_p e$: output $= \dfrac{L}{1+L}\,r$, where $L$ is the loop gain.
- Sensitivity to plant changes, the effect of disturbances and the time constant all shrink by $1 + L$.
- P control alone leaves a steady-state error of $\dfrac{1}{1+L}$; integral action removes it.
- Gain is limited by stability, actuator saturation and sensor noise.
