A robot arm with six joints has six angles and six velocities. Writing twelve separate equations would be unreadable, so robotics packs numbers into **vectors** and the relationships between them into **matrices**. From the next lesson on, almost every equation in this course is written that way. This lesson covers the basics you need to read them.

## Vectors

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, written as a column:

$$x = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

You can read it two ways, and both are useful:

- **Geometrically:** an arrow from the origin to the point $(2, 3)$, with a length and a direction.
- **As a container:** a set of quantities that belong together, such as an arm's two joint angles, or a system's position and velocity. They need not be the same kind of quantity.

Two operations are defined on vectors. **Adding** them adds matching entries; **scaling** by a number multiplies every entry:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\qquad 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

The **dot product** of two vectors multiplies matching entries and sums the results, giving a single number: $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1\end{bmatrix} = 2 - 3 = -1$. It is zero when two arrows are perpendicular. The **length** of a vector is $\sqrt{x \cdot x}$, here $\sqrt{4 + 9} = 3.61$.

## Matrices

A matrix is a rectangular grid of numbers. This one has 2 rows and 2 columns:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

The most important thing a matrix does is **multiply a vector to produce a new vector**. Each entry of the result is the dot product of one row of the matrix with the vector:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 3 \\ 1\cdot 1 + 3\cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}$$

So a matrix is a **function** that takes a vector in and gives a vector out. It is a special kind of function, a *linear* one: it can stretch, rotate, reflect and shear space, but it always keeps straight lines straight and the origin fixed.

A rotation is the classic example. This matrix rotates any 2-D vector counter-clockwise by the angle $\theta$:

$$R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$

With $\theta = 90°$ it sends $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ to $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$: the arrow pointing along $x$ now points along $y$. Module 3 is built on matrices like this one.

## Multiplying matrices: doing one thing after another

If $B$ transforms a vector and then $A$ transforms the result, the combined effect is a single matrix, the product $AB$. Note the order: the matrix written on the **right** acts **first**.

Entry by entry, the rule is "row of the left matrix dotted with column of the right matrix".

One thing here is different from ordinary numbers, and it matters constantly in robotics: **order matters**. In general

$$AB \neq BA$$

Rotating then shifting an object does not put it in the same place as shifting then rotating. With the matrix $A$ above and $B = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$:

$$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\qquad BA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

## Three special things

- The **identity matrix** $I$ has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. It leaves every vector unchanged: $Ix = x$. It plays the role of the number 1.
- The **transpose** $A^T$ swaps rows and columns. It turns a column vector into a row vector.
- The **inverse** $A^{-1}$ undoes what $A$ does: $A^{-1}A = I$. Not every matrix has one.

For a 2×2 matrix there is a formula for the inverse:

$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$

The quantity $ad - bc$ is the **determinant**. Geometrically it is the factor by which the matrix scales areas. If it is zero, the matrix flattens the plane onto a line, information is lost, and no inverse exists. Such a matrix is called **singular**. In robotics a singular matrix usually signals a physical problem, such as an arm stretched out straight that can no longer move its hand in some direction.

## Solving systems of equations

Two equations in two unknowns,

$$\begin{aligned} 2x_1 + x_2 &= 5 \\ x_1 + 3x_2 &= 10 \end{aligned}$$

are one matrix equation, $Ax = b$, with the $A$ from above and $b = \begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 10\end{bmatrix}$. The question "which $x$ does $A$ send to $b$?" is answered by the inverse: $x = A^{-1}b$. Here the determinant is $2\cdot 3 - 1\cdot 1 = 5$, and the solution is $x = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$, matching the multiplication worked out earlier.

## In Python

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 1],
              [1, 3]])
b = np.array([5, 10])

A @ np.array([1, 3])     # [ 5, 10]   matrix times vector
np.linalg.solve(A, b)    # [1., 3.]   solves A x = b
np.linalg.det(A)         # 5.0
np.linalg.inv(A)         # [[ 0.6, -0.2], [-0.2,  0.4]]
A.T                      # transpose
```

Prefer `np.linalg.solve(A, b)` to `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b`. It is faster and more accurate, and it is the habit professional code follows.

=== frc ===

Field-oriented drive is a matrix multiplication. The driver's joystick gives a velocity in *field* coordinates; the robot needs it in its *own* coordinates. The conversion is the rotation matrix $R(-\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the gyro heading, applied to the velocity vector. That is what `ChassisSpeeds.fromFieldRelativeSpeeds` computes. Swerve kinematics is another: one matrix maps the chassis velocity $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$ to the eight numbers describing the four module velocities, and its (pseudo-)inverse maps measured module velocities back to chassis motion for odometry.

=== summary ===

- A vector is a column of numbers: an arrow, or a bundle of related quantities.
- A matrix times a vector gives a new vector; a matrix is a linear function.
- $AB$ means "apply $B$, then $A$". In general $AB \neq BA$.
- $A^{-1}$ undoes $A$ and exists only if the determinant is non-zero.
- $Ax = b$ is a system of linear equations; solve it with `np.linalg.solve`.

? A 2×2 matrix has a determinant of zero. What does that tell you?
- It is the identity matrix
- It rotates vectors without changing their length
* It has no inverse, because it squashes the plane onto a line
- Every entry is zero
> A zero determinant means areas are scaled to zero. Different inputs map to the same output, so the mapping cannot be undone.
