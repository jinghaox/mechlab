The first-order system is the simplest dynamic system there is, and the most useful. A motor coming up to speed, a capacitor charging, a heater warming up and a filtered sensor reading all behave the same way. Learn this one response well and you will recognize it for the rest of the course.

## Two systems, one equation

**An RC circuit.** A voltage $u$ drives a resistor $R$ in series with a capacitor $C$. The output $y$ is the capacitor voltage. The current through the resistor is $(u - y)/R$, and that current charges the capacitor: $C\dot{y} = (u - y)/R$. Rearranged:

$$RC\,\dot{y} + y = u$$

**A motor's speed.** A motor with rotor inertia $J$ spins at speed $\omega$. The applied voltage $u$ produces a torque $Ku$, and friction produces an opposing torque $b\,\omega$. Newton's law for rotation, torque = inertia × angular acceleration, gives $J\dot{\omega} = Ku - b\,\omega$. Rearranged:

$$\frac{J}{b}\,\dot{\omega} + \omega = \frac{K}{b}\,u$$

These are the same equation with different letters. The **standard form** of a first-order system is

$$\tau\,\dot{y} + y = K\,u$$

with just two parameters:

| Parameter | Name | RC circuit | Motor |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\tau$ | time constant [s] | $RC$ | $J/b$ |
| $K$ | steady-state gain | $1$ | $K/b$ |

## The step response

Start at rest, $y(0) = 0$, and at $t = 0$ switch the input from zero to a constant $u_0$. The solution is

$$y(t) = K u_0 \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$

Read off what the two parameters do:

- **$K$ sets where it ends up.** As $t \to \infty$ the exponential vanishes and $y \to K u_0$. You can see this without solving anything: in steady state nothing changes, so $\dot{y} = 0$ and the equation reduces to $y = Ku$.
- **$\tau$ sets how fast it gets there.** It does not affect the final value at all.

The exponential gives a fixed timetable that is worth memorizing:

| Time | Fraction of the final value |
|---|---|
| $\tau$ | 63.2% |
| $2\tau$ | 86.5% |
| $3\tau$ | 95.0% |
| $4\tau$ | 98.2% |
| $5\tau$ | 99.3% |

Engineers say a first-order system has "settled" after about $4\tau$ to $5\tau$.

One more useful fact: the **initial slope** is $K u_0/\tau$. If the response kept going at its initial rate it would reach the final value in exactly one time constant. It does not, because the slope keeps shrinking as the output approaches its target. That self-limiting behaviour is the essence of a first-order system: the rate of change is proportional to the distance still to go.

## The free response

Now do the opposite: the system is at $y_0$ and the input is switched off. With $u = 0$ the equation is $\tau\dot{y} = -y$, and

$$y(t) = y_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$$

Same time constant, same timetable, mirrored: down to 36.8% after $\tau$, 5% after $3\tau$. A motor coasting to a stop does this.

## Reading τ and K from data

Because the response has such a recognizable shape, you can identify a first-order system from a single experiment:

1. Apply a step input and record the output.
2. The final value divided by the step size is $K$.
3. The time taken to reach 63.2% of the final value is $\tau$.

This is the simplest case of **system identification**, and you will do it in the lab. On real hardware it is how you find parameters that no datasheet gives you, such as the friction in your particular gearbox.

## Linearity

Double the input and the output doubles at every instant; the time constant does not change. The response to two inputs applied together is the sum of the responses to each one alone. This property, **superposition**, is what makes a system *linear*, and it is the reason the analysis tools of the next lessons work at all. Real systems are linear only within limits: a motor's speed stops rising once the driver saturates at full voltage.

The WPILib feedforward model $V = k_S + k_V\,v + k_A\,a$ **is** this lesson's motor equation. Ignore the static friction term $k_S$ and rearrange:

$$\frac{k_A}{k_V}\,\dot{v} + v = \frac{1}{k_V}\,V$$

So a drivetrain or flywheel is a first-order system with gain $1/k_V$ and time constant $\tau = k_A/k_V$. When SysId reports $k_V$ and $k_A$ for a mechanism, it has measured exactly the two numbers from this lesson. A flywheel with $k_V = 0.12$ V·s/rad and $k_A = 0.06$ V·s²/rad has $\tau = 0.5$ s, so it needs about 2 seconds to spin up on feedforward alone. That delay is why shooters add feedback.

=== summary ===

- Standard form: $\tau\,\dot{y} + y = K\,u$.
- Step response: $y(t) = K u_0\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. Free response: $y(t) = y_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$.
- $K$ fixes the final value; $\tau$ fixes the speed. 63% at $\tau$, 95% at $3\tau$, settled by $4\tau$ to $5\tau$.
- Motor speed: $\tau = J/b$, gain $= K/b$. More inertia is slower; more friction is faster but with a lower top speed.
- Identify $K$ and $\tau$ from one step test.
