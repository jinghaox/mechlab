Every controller in this module so far was designed as if it watched the plant continuously. In reality it runs on a processor, and the processor looks at the world in snapshots: read the sensor, compute, write the output, wait for the next tick. This lesson covers what sampling does to a control loop, how to implement PID so it behaves the same at any rate, and how fast is fast enough.

## What a digital controller actually does

Once every sample period $T$:

1. **Sample.** Read the sensor. This is the value $y_k$, a snapshot at time $kT$.
2. **Compute.** Run the control law to get $u_k$.
3. **Hold.** Write $u_k$ to the actuator, where it stays constant until the next sample. This is called a **zero-order hold**.

Between samples the controller is blind and its output is frozen. Two things follow.

## Effect 1: the hold is a delay

A smooth control signal becomes a staircase. The staircase, on average, lags the smooth signal it approximates by half a step. So a zero-order hold behaves approximately like a time delay of

$$T_{\text{delay}} \approx \frac{T}{2}$$

If the computation takes a significant part of the period, and the output is only written at the next tick, add up to another full $T$. From lesson 2.5 you know exactly what a delay costs:

$$\text{phase margin lost} \approx \omega_c \cdot \frac{T}{2}$$

This single formula explains most of digital control. A slow loop is a loop with a built-in delay.

## Effect 2: aliasing

Sampling cannot distinguish a fast signal from a slow one that happens to pass through the same sample points. A signal at frequency $f$ sampled at rate $f_s$ is indistinguishable from one at $f_s - f$. The limit is the **Nyquist frequency**, $f_s/2$: anything above it is misread as a lower frequency. This is *aliasing*, the same effect that makes wheels appear to turn backwards on film.

For control it means high-frequency vibration or electrical noise can appear in your samples as a slow, plausible-looking signal that the controller will then try to "correct". The defence is an analog low-pass filter before the sampler (an *anti-aliasing filter*), or sampling much faster than the loop needs and filtering digitally. Lesson 6.5 returns to this.

## How fast is fast enough?

Using the delay formula: to lose no more than about 5° to 10° of phase margin, you need $\omega_c T/2 < 0.1$ to $0.2$ rad. That works out to the standard rule of thumb:

> **Sample 10 to 20 times faster than the closed-loop bandwidth** (with both in the same units).

The lab runs the PID arm controller from lesson 2.2 at different rates. Its bandwidth is roughly 15 to 20 rad/s, about 3 Hz:

| Control rate | Period | Overshoot | Settling time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1000 Hz | 1 ms | 4.2% | 0.59 s |
| 100 Hz | 10 ms | 3.2% | 0.59 s |
| 50 Hz | 20 ms | 2.8% | 0.66 s |
| 20 Hz | 50 ms | 4.0% | 0.85 s |
| 10 Hz | 100 ms | sustained oscillation | |
| 5 Hz | 200 ms | sustained oscillation | |

From 1000 Hz down to 50 Hz almost nothing changes: once you are comfortably above the rule, extra speed buys nothing. At 20 Hz, about 7 times the bandwidth, the response is visibly slower to settle. At 10 Hz the half-sample delay of 50 ms costs roughly 50° of phase at crossover, the margin is gone, and the arm oscillates indefinitely.

The rule also cuts the other way. If you are stuck with a fixed sample rate, it **limits the bandwidth you may ask for**. A 50 Hz loop can support a bandwidth of about 3 to 5 Hz, no matter how the gains are chosen.

## Implementing PID so it is rate-independent

The discrete PID replaces the integral with a sum and the derivative with a difference:

$$u_k = K_p e_k + K_i \sum_{i=0}^{k} e_i\,T + K_d\,\frac{e_k - e_{k-1}}{T}$$

The factors of $T$ are the point. With them, $K_i$ and $K_d$ keep their continuous-time meaning and units, and the controller behaves the same when the sample rate changes. That is how the lab could use identical gains from 1000 Hz to 20 Hz.

A common mistake is to leave them out:

```python
integral += error                    # wrong: depends on the loop rate
derivative = error - previous_error  # wrong: depends on the loop rate
```

This works, but the *effective* $K_i$ is now proportional to the loop rate and the effective $K_d$ inversely proportional. Change the rate, or miss a deadline, and the tuning changes. Always carry $T$ explicitly, and if the period can vary, measure the actual elapsed time each cycle.

## Timing quality matters too

A control loop assumes its period is constant. Variation in the period is called **jitter**. It corrupts the derivative directly (the difference is divided by a $T$ that was not the real one) and behaves like a randomly varying delay. This is why control code runs from a hardware timer interrupt or a real-time scheduler, never from a loop that simply runs as fast as it can alongside other work.

Resolution interacts with sampling as well. An encoder with a resolution of $\Delta\theta$ gives a velocity estimate with a resolution of $\Delta\theta/T$. Sampling **faster** makes the velocity estimate **coarser**. At very high rates each sample sees zero or one encoder count, and the differentiated signal is mostly noise. Lesson 6.1 covers the remedies.

## The z-plane, briefly

Sampling a continuous system with a pole at $s$ gives a discrete system with a pole at

$$z = e^{sT}$$

A stable continuous pole, with negative real part, maps to $|z| < 1$. So for discrete systems the stability region is the **inside of the unit circle**, where for continuous systems it was the left half-plane. The pole at $s = -5$ from the motor becomes $z = 0.995$ at 1 kHz and $z = 0.607$ at 10 Hz. You will not need to design in the z-plane in this course, but you will see these numbers in library output, and now you know how to read them: closer to 1 means slower relative to the sample rate.

The roboRIO's `TimedRobot` calls your `periodic()` methods every 20 ms, so a loop closed there runs at 50 Hz. By this lesson's rule, that supports a closed-loop bandwidth of about 3 to 5 Hz. === summary ===

- A digital controller samples, computes and holds. Between samples it is blind.
- The zero-order hold acts like a delay of $T/2$, costing $\omega_c T/2$ radians of phase margin.
- Sample at 10–20 times the closed-loop bandwidth. A fixed rate caps the achievable bandwidth.
- Discrete PID: `integral += e * T`, `derivative = (e - e_prev) / T`. Keep $T$ explicit so the gains are rate-independent.
- Signals above the Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$ alias to lower frequencies.
- Sampled poles are at $z = e^{sT}$; stable means inside the unit circle.
