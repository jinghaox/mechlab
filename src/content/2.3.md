"Make it respond well" is not a specification. Before tuning a controller you need numbers that say what *good* means, and after tuning you need to measure whether you got there. This lesson defines the standard step-response specifications, connects them to the $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$ of Module 1, and then gives you two practical tuning procedures.

## Four numbers that describe a step response

Command a sudden change in the reference and record the output. Four measurements summarize the result:

| Specification | Definition | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Rise time** $t_r$ | time to go from 10% to 90% of the step | how fast the system responds |
| **Overshoot** $M_p$ | peak excursion beyond the final value, as a percentage of the step | how oscillatory it is |
| **Settling time** $t_s$ | time after which the output stays within ±2% of the final value | when it is actually finished |
| **Steady-state error** $e_{ss}$ | difference between reference and final value | how accurate it is at rest |

Different jobs weight these differently. A machine tool must have zero overshoot, because overshoot cuts into the part. A camera gimbal tolerates some overshoot in exchange for speed. A robot arm near a person needs a bounded speed more than a short rise time.

## Reading them from ζ and ω_n

Many closed loops behave approximately like the second-order system of lesson 1.2, and then the specifications follow from its two parameters:

$$M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \qquad t_s \approx \frac{4}{\zeta\omega_n} \qquad t_r \approx \frac{1.8}{\omega_n}$$

These turn a specification into a region of the s-plane where the closed-loop poles must lie:

- "Overshoot below 5%" means $\zeta > 0.7$: the poles must lie within about 45° of the negative real axis.
- "Settle within 0.5 s" means $\zeta\omega_n > 8$: the poles must lie to the left of $-8$.
- "Rise in under 0.1 s" means $\omega_n > 18$: the poles must be at least that far from the origin.

Design is then a matter of choosing gains that put the poles in the allowed region. You cannot have everything: faster needs larger $\omega_n$, which needs more gain, which needs more actuator effort and amplifies more noise.

## Tuning by hand

When you have the system in front of you and no model, this sequence works for most position loops:

1. **Start with everything at zero**, and command small steps.
2. **Raise $K_p$** until the response is fast but clearly oscillatory.
3. **Raise $K_d$** until the oscillation is gone. If the response becomes sluggish or the actuator gets noisy, you have gone too far.
4. **Repeat** steps 2 and 3. More damping allows more $K_p$.
5. **Add $K_i$ last**, and only as much as needed to remove steady-state error in a reasonable time. Too much brings the overshoot back.

Change one gain at a time, by a factor of about 1.5 to 2, and look at a plot after each change. Tuning by ear and by watching the mechanism is much slower than tuning from a recorded step response.

## Ziegler–Nichols: tuning from one experiment

In 1942 Ziegler and Nichols published a recipe that needs only a single test:

1. Use P control only. Raise $K_p$ until the loop oscillates steadily, neither growing nor decaying.
2. Record that gain, the **ultimate gain** $K_u$, and the period of the oscillation, $T_u$.
3. Set the gains from this table:

| Controller | $K_p$ | $K_i$ | $K_d$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | $0.5\,K_u$ | | |
| PI | $0.45\,K_u$ | $0.54\,K_u/T_u$ | |
| PID | $0.6\,K_u$ | $1.2\,K_u/T_u$ | $0.075\,K_u T_u$ |

The appeal is that $K_u$ and $T_u$ capture where the plant runs out of stability, without a model. The weakness is that the rules were designed for quick disturbance rejection in process plants, and they accept a lot of oscillation to get it.

The lab applies the recipe to a motor position loop with $K_u = 1.1$ and $T_u = 0.397$ s, and compares it with a PD controller tuned by hand:

| Design | Rise time | Overshoot | Settling time |
|---|---|---|---|
| P, $K_p = 0.2$ | 0.188 s | 39% | 1.99 s |
| P, $K_p = 0.5$ | 0.106 s | 68% | 2.99 s |
| Ziegler–Nichols PID | 0.075 s | 66% | 1.64 s |
| Hand-tuned PD | 0.071 s | 17% | 0.34 s |

The Ziegler–Nichols result is fast and stable, but its overshoot would be unacceptable for a robot. Treat it as what it is: **a starting point**, usually too aggressive, from which you back off $K_p$ and $K_i$ and add $K_d$. Never bring real hardware to sustained oscillation near its limits unless you are sure it can survive that.

## A warning about trade-offs

The two P-only rows show a pattern you will see again and again: raising the gain made the rise time shorter and the settling time **longer**. Fast is not the same as finished. If you tune by watching rise time alone you will tune yourself into oscillation.

=== summary ===

- Rise time (speed), overshoot (oscillation), settling time (completion), steady-state error (accuracy).
- For a second-order response: $M_p$ depends on $\zeta$ only; $t_s \approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n)$; $t_r \approx 1.8/\omega_n$.
- Hand tuning: raise P until oscillatory, add D to damp, repeat, add I last and sparingly.
- Ziegler–Nichols: find $K_u$ and $T_u$, then $K_p = 0.6K_u$, $K_i = 1.2K_u/T_u$, $K_d = 0.075K_uT_u$. A starting point, typically too aggressive.
- A shorter rise time often comes with a longer settling time.
