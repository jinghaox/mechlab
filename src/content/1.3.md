For the first- and second-order systems of the last two lessons there are exact formulas. For a robot arm with six joints, friction and contact there are none. The only general way to find out what a system does is to **integrate its differential equation numerically**. Every simulator, MuJoCo included, is built on this, and so is every control loop that runs on a microcontroller. This lesson shows how it works and how it fails.

## The setup

Write the system as a first-order equation for its state:

$$\dot{x} = f(x)$$

A second-order equation fits this form if you stack position and velocity into one state vector. For an ideal pendulum, $\ddot{\theta} = -\tfrac{g}{L}\sin\theta$ becomes

$$x = \begin{bmatrix}\theta \\ \omega\end{bmatrix}, \qquad f(x) = \begin{bmatrix}\omega \\ -\tfrac{g}{L}\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}$$

The function $f$ answers one question: *given the state right now, how fast is each component changing?* An integrator's job is to turn that into the state a short time $h$ later. The time $h$ is the **step size**.

## Euler's method

The simplest idea: assume the rate of change stays constant over the step.

$$x_{k+1} = x_k + h\,f(x_k)$$

It is one line of code, and it is wrong in a predictable way: the rate does *not* stay constant during the step, so each step lands slightly off. The error made in one step is proportional to $h^2$. Over a fixed simulation time you take $1/h$ times as many steps, so the accumulated error is proportional to $h$. Euler's method is therefore called **first-order accurate**: halve the step and you halve the error.

That is a poor trade. To get ten times the accuracy you must do ten times the work.

## Runge-Kutta 4

RK4 takes a smarter step. It samples the slope four times, at the start, twice near the middle and once at the end of the step, then moves along a weighted average:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_1 &= f(x_k) \\
k_2 &= f\!\left(x_k + \tfrac{h}{2}k_1\right) \\
k_3 &= f\!\left(x_k + \tfrac{h}{2}k_2\right) \\
k_4 &= f\!\left(x_k + h\,k_3\right) \\
x_{k+1} &= x_k + \tfrac{h}{6}\left(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step costs four evaluations of $f$ instead of one, but the accumulated error is proportional to $h^4$. Halve the step and the error drops by a factor of 16. For smooth systems this is an enormous gain: in the lab, with the same step of 0.01 s, RK4 is about a million times more accurate than Euler.

## Stability: when the simulation blows up

Accuracy is one concern. The other is more dramatic: with too large a step, the numerical solution can grow without bound even though the real system is perfectly well behaved.

Take the first-order system $\dot{y} = -y/\tau$. One Euler step gives

$$y_{k+1} = \left(1 - \frac{h}{\tau}\right)y_k$$

Each step multiplies $y$ by the same factor. The true solution decays, so the numerical one should too, which requires that factor to be between $-1$ and $1$:

$$h < 2\tau$$

With $\tau = 0.2$ s, a step of 0.3 s still decays (while alternating in sign, which is wrong but bounded). A step of 0.5 s multiplies the state by $-1.5$ every step and the simulation explodes.

The practical rule that follows: **the step must be small compared with the fastest time constant in the system**, even if you only care about slow behaviour. A system with both very fast and very slow dynamics is called **stiff**, and it forces a tiny step on explicit methods like Euler and RK4. Stiff contact between rigid bodies is the usual reason robot simulations need steps of a millisecond or two.

For an *undamped* oscillator the news is worse: plain Euler is unstable at **every** step size. It adds a little energy at every step, so a frictionless pendulum swings higher and higher. A smaller step only slows the growth.

## What real tools do

- **`solve_ivp`** uses an *adaptive* method. It estimates its own error and shrinks or grows the step to meet a tolerance. Excellent for offline analysis.
- **MuJoCo** uses a fixed step, because a controller needs to run at a fixed rate. Its default "Euler" integrator is a *semi-implicit* variant: it updates velocity first, then uses the **new** velocity to update position. That small change makes it far better behaved for oscillating systems than the textbook method above. This is why the Euler run in Lab 0.3 drifted only slightly, while the plain Euler you write in this lab goes badly wrong.
- **Microcontrollers** usually run plain Euler-style updates inside the control loop, at a rate chosen to be much faster than the plant.

=== frc ===

Your robot code integrates every 20 ms. Odometry is the clearest case: each loop it takes the measured wheel speeds and adds "velocity × 0.02 s" to the pose estimate, which is an Euler step. The errors behave as this lesson predicts: the pose drifts, and it drifts faster when the robot turns quickly, because the constant-velocity assumption is worst then. WPILib reduces the error by assuming the robot follows a constant-curvature arc during each step (the `Twist2d` "pose exponential") rather than a straight line. That is a better integrator, in the same spirit as RK4.

=== summary ===

- Write the system as $\dot{x} = f(x)$; an integrator advances $x$ by a step $h$.
- Euler: $x_{k+1} = x_k + h f(x_k)$. Error proportional to $h$.
- RK4: four slope samples per step. Error proportional to $h^4$.
- Stability needs $h$ small relative to the **fastest** dynamics; for Euler on a first-order system, $h < 2\tau$.
- Plain Euler adds energy to undamped oscillators. Semi-implicit Euler (MuJoCo's default) behaves much better.
