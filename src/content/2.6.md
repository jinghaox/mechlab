The textbook PID of lesson 2.2 is five lines. An industrial PID implementation is a few hundred. The difference is a handful of fixes for problems that appear as soon as a controller meets real hardware: motors that saturate, sensors that are noisy, and references that jump. This lesson covers the four that matter most. Each one is demonstrated on the MuJoCo arm.

## 1. Feedforward: use what you already know

Feedback waits for an error before it acts. But some of what the motor must do is known in advance. To hold the arm at angle $\theta$, the motor must supply $2.94\sin\theta$ N·m against gravity. There is no reason to wait for the arm to sag and the integral to wind up before providing it.

**Feedforward** computes the expected command directly from the reference and adds it to the feedback:

$$u = \underbrace{u_{ff}(r, \dot{r}, \ddot{r})}_{\text{model-based guess}} + \underbrace{K_p e + K_d \dot{e}}_{\text{correction}}$$

For the arm, inverting the equation of motion gives the torque needed to follow a desired motion exactly:

$$u_{ff} = I\,\ddot{r} + b\,\dot{r} + mgd\,\sin r$$

The three terms supply the torque to accelerate the inertia, to overcome friction, and to hold against gravity. The lab has the arm follow a smooth back-and-forth motion between 0° and 90°:

| Controller | RMS tracking error |
|---|---|
| PD only | 5.05° |
| PID | 1.68° |
| PD + gravity feedforward | 1.81° |
| PD + full feedforward | 0.00° |

With a perfect model, feedforward alone does the whole job and feedback has nothing left to correct. Real models are never perfect, so the feedback loop stays, but it now handles only the *model error* instead of the entire command. The division of labour is the key design principle:

> **Feedforward for what you can predict. Feedback for what you cannot.**

Feedforward has another virtue: it is not in the loop, so it **cannot make the system unstable**. A wrong feedforward gives a tracking error, never an oscillation.

## 2. Integrator windup

The integral term assumes the actuator does what it is told. When the actuator **saturates**, that assumption breaks.

Suppose the arm is commanded through a large move and the motor hits its torque limit. The error stays large for a long time, because the motor cannot go any faster. Meanwhile the integral keeps accumulating, growing far beyond any value the motor could deliver. When the arm finally arrives, that enormous integral is still there. The error must go *negative* and stay negative long enough to drain it. The arm overshoots badly.

This is **integrator windup**. In the lab the motor is limited to 3.5 N·m, barely above the 2.94 N·m that gravity needs:

| | Overshoot | Settling time | Peak of integral term |
|---|---|---|---|
| No protection | 37.5% | 1.69 s | 15.6 N·m |
| Anti-windup | 0% | 0.52 s | 2.9 N·m |

Without protection the integral term reached 15.6 N·m on a motor that can deliver 3.5. The fix used here is **conditional integration** (clamping): stop integrating whenever the output is saturated and the error would push it further into saturation.

```python
saturated = u != u_unsaturated
pushing_further = error * (u_unsaturated - u) > 0
if not (saturated and pushing_further):
    integral += error * dt
```

Windup happens in any loop with integral action and a limited actuator, which is all of them. It is the most common cause of "the controller works for small moves and goes crazy on big ones".

## 3. Derivative and noise

Differentiation amplifies whatever changes quickly, and nothing changes more quickly than sensor noise. A measurement error of $\pm\epsilon$ that flips between samples produces a derivative of about $2\epsilon/\Delta t$. At a 1 kHz loop rate, noise of 0.1° becomes a "velocity" of hundreds of degrees per second, multiplied by $K_d$ and sent to the motor.

The cure is to low-pass filter the derivative, using the first-order filter of lesson 1.1 with time constant $\tau_d$:

| Derivative filter | Torque noise (RMS) | Resulting angle jitter |
|---|---|---|
| none | 4.2 N·m | 0.48° |
| 5 ms | 0.65 N·m | 0.012° |
| 20 ms | 0.21 N·m | 0.014° |
| 100 ms | 0.07 N·m | 0.016° |

Unfiltered, the motor is being commanded with 4.2 N·m of pure noise on an 8 N·m actuator. It would run hot, sound rough and wear its gears. Notice that the unfiltered loop also *shakes the arm* by 0.48°, five times the sensor noise it started with.

The filter is not free. It is a lag inside the loop, and from lesson 2.5 a lag costs phase margin. A heavy filter removes the damping the D term was there to provide. A common choice is to place the filter corner a factor of 5 to 10 above the loop's crossover frequency.

## 4. Derivative kick

When the setpoint jumps, the error jumps with it, and the derivative of a jump is, for one sample, enormous. In the lab a setpoint change from 45° to 90° produced:

| Derivative computed from | Largest torque command |
|---|---|
| the error $e = r - y$ | 1587 N·m |
| the measurement $-y$ only | 15.7 N·m |

A request for 1587 N·m from an 8 N·m motor is a one-sample spike that slams the actuator into its limit. The standard fix is **derivative on measurement**: differentiate $-y$ instead of $e$. Since $e = r - y$, the two are identical whenever the reference is constant, so the damping is unchanged; only the spike disappears.

There is a trade-off, and the feedforward lab exposes it. When the reference is *moving* smoothly, derivative-on-measurement resists the commanded motion itself, as a brake would. The same PD controller with full feedforward gave a tracking error of 0.00° with derivative-on-error and 9.10° with derivative-on-measurement. So:

- For **setpoint steps** (regulation): differentiate the measurement.
- For **smooth trajectories** (tracking): differentiate the error, or equivalently add $K_d\dot{r}$ as a feedforward term.

Better still, avoid setpoint steps altogether by feeding the controller a smooth motion profile, which lesson 4.5 covers.

=== frc ===

All four fixes are in WPILib, under these names. **Feedforward** is `SimpleMotorFeedforward`, `ArmFeedforward` and `ElevatorFeedforward`: the $k_S$, $k_G$, $k_V$ and $k_A$ terms are friction, gravity, velocity and acceleration, the same pieces as $u_{ff}$ above. **Anti-windup** is `PIDController.setIntegratorRange()` and `setIZone()`, which clamp the integral or enable it only near the setpoint. **Derivative noise** is why the D gain on a velocity loop is so often left at zero. And **avoiding steps** is exactly what `ProfiledPIDController` and `TrapezoidProfile` are for: they convert a jump in the goal into a smooth setpoint with matching velocity, which you can then feed to the feedforward.

=== summary ===

- **Feedforward** supplies the predictable part of the command: $u_{ff} = I\ddot{r} + b\dot{r} + mgd\sin r$. It cannot destabilize the loop.
- **Windup:** a saturated actuator lets the integral grow without limit, causing large overshoot. Stop integrating while saturated.
- **Derivative noise:** filter the D term; put the filter corner 5–10 times above crossover.
- **Derivative kick:** differentiate the measurement for setpoint steps, the error for smooth trajectories.
- Give the controller smooth references whenever you can.
