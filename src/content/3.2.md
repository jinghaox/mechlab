Rotation matrices are safe but bulky: nine numbers for three degrees of freedom, with six constraints to maintain. Euler angles are compact but break at gimbal lock. Two more representations fill the gap. **Axis-angle** is the easiest to picture, and the **unit quaternion** is what almost all robotics software stores internally. This lesson shows what a quaternion is, without mystery, and why it is so widely used.

## Axis-angle: every rotation is one turn

A theorem of Euler says that any orientation, however it was reached, can be produced from the reference orientation by a **single rotation** about some axis. So an orientation can be described by

- a unit vector $\hat{n}$, the axis, and
- an angle $\theta$, how far to turn about it.

For example, turning 90° about $x$ and then 90° about $z$ is the same as one turn of 120° about the diagonal axis $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}$.

Often the two are packed into one 3-vector, $\theta\hat{n}$, called a **rotation vector**. It is a natural way to express a *small* rotation or an angular velocity: $\omega$, the angular velocity vector, is exactly this, an axis and a rate. Axis-angle is less convenient for combining rotations, which is where quaternions come in.

## The quaternion

A **unit quaternion** stores an axis-angle rotation in four numbers:

$$q = \begin{bmatrix} w \\ x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) \\ \hat{n}\,\sin(\theta/2) \end{bmatrix}$$

The first number, $w$, is the *scalar part*; the other three form the *vector part*, which points along the rotation axis. That is all a quaternion is: the axis, scaled by the sine of half the angle, plus the cosine of half the angle.

Some values to recognize:

| Rotation | Quaternion $(w, x, y, z)$ |
|---|---|
| none | $(1, 0, 0, 0)$ |
| 180° about $x$ | $(0, 1, 0, 0)$ |
| 90° about $z$ | $(0.707, 0, 0, 0.707)$ |
| 120° about $(1,1,1)$ | $(0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5)$ |

Since $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$, the four numbers always satisfy $w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$. That is the meaning of "unit", and it is the single constraint that leaves three degrees of freedom.

## Using them

**Composition.** Two rotations combine by the *quaternion product*. Writing each as a scalar and a vector, $p = (p_w, \vec{p})$ and $q = (q_w, \vec{q})$:

$$p \otimes q = \left(p_w q_w - \vec{p}\cdot\vec{q},\;\; p_w\vec{q} + q_w\vec{p} + \vec{p}\times\vec{q}\right)$$

As with matrices, $p \otimes q$ means "apply $q$ first, then $p$", and the order matters. The cross product is what makes it non-commutative. In the lab, multiplying the quaternions for 90° about $z$ and 90° about $x$ gives $(0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5)$: the 120° turn about the diagonal mentioned above.

**Inverse.** The opposite rotation is the same angle about the opposite axis, so just negate the vector part: $q^{-1} = (w, -x, -y, -z)$. This is called the *conjugate*.

**Rotating a vector.** Treat the vector $v$ as a quaternion with zero scalar part and sandwich it: $v' = q \otimes (0, v) \otimes q^{-1}$.

In practice you will call a library for all three. What matters is knowing what the operations mean.

## Why software prefers them

- **No gimbal lock.** Every orientation has a well-behaved quaternion. There are no special angles.
- **Compact and cheap.** Four numbers and one constraint, against nine numbers and six constraints.
- **Easy to keep valid.** Numerical error slowly spoils any representation. A drifting rotation matrix must be re-orthogonalized, which is fiddly. A drifting quaternion is fixed by dividing by its length.
- **Good interpolation.** See below.

## Two things that trip people up

**The double cover.** A rotation of $\theta$ about $\hat{n}$ and a rotation of $\theta + 360°$ about the same axis are the same orientation, but the half-angle formulas give $q$ for one and $-q$ for the other. So **$q$ and $-q$ represent the same orientation.** This matters whenever you compare or subtract quaternions: two nearly identical orientations can have nearly opposite quaternions. Before computing an orientation error, flip the sign of one quaternion if their dot product is negative.

**The storage order.** There is no agreement on whether the scalar part comes first or last:

| Library | Order |
|---|---|
| MuJoCo, Eigen (constructor), WPILib | $(w, x, y, z)$ |
| SciPy, ROS, Unity | $(x, y, z, w)$ |

The lab prints the same 90° turn from MuJoCo as `[0.707, 0, 0, 0.707]` and from SciPy as `[0, 0, 0.707, 0.707]`. Those happen to be easy to tell apart. A general quaternion passed between libraries in the wrong order gives a valid-looking but completely wrong orientation, with no error message. Check the convention every time data crosses a library boundary.

## Interpolating orientations

Suppose a robot's hand must turn smoothly from one orientation to another. Interpolating the three Euler angles linearly seems natural, and it is wrong: the hand takes a wandering path and turns at an uneven rate.

The right tool is **slerp**, spherical linear interpolation, which turns at a constant rate about the single fixed axis that connects the two orientations. The lab compares the two for a pair of orientations 99.9° apart:

| Fraction of the way | Slerp: angle turned | Euler interpolation: angle turned |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 10.0° | 24.5° |
| 0.5 | 49.9° | 96.3° |
| 0.8 | 79.9° | 109.9° |
| 1.0 | 99.9° | 99.9° |

Slerp advances 10° every tenth, as it should. The Euler path has already turned 96° at the halfway point, overshoots to 110°, and then comes back. Both arrive at the same place; only one gets there sensibly.

## Which representation when

| Representation | Numbers | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Rotation matrix | 9 | transforming points, composing frames, derivations |
| Quaternion | 4 | storing, integrating and interpolating orientation |
| Axis-angle / rotation vector | 3 | small rotations, orientation *errors*, angular velocity |
| Euler angles | 3 | display and human input |

=== frc ===

WPILib's `Rotation3d` holds a `Quaternion` in $(w, x, y, z)$ order, and `Pose3d.interpolate()` uses the kind of constant-rate interpolation described here. The place you are most likely to hit this lesson's traps is AprilTag vision. Tag poses in the field layout file are stored as quaternions, the camera pipeline may report orientation in its own axis convention, and combining them requires the conventions to match exactly. When a vision pose estimate is wrong by a consistent 90° or 180°, or is mirrored, suspect a frame or ordering convention before you suspect the camera.

=== summary ===

- Any orientation is one rotation by $\theta$ about an axis $\hat{n}$.
- Unit quaternion: $q = (\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\ \hat{n}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2})$, with $w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$.
- Compose with the quaternion product (order matters); invert by negating the vector part.
- No gimbal lock; renormalize by dividing by the length.
- $q$ and $-q$ are the same orientation.
- Check the storage order: MuJoCo uses $(w, x, y, z)$, SciPy and ROS use $(x, y, z, w)$.
- Interpolate orientations with slerp, never by interpolating Euler angles.
