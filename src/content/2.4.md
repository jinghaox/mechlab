Lesson 2.1 ended with a warning: push the gain too high and a feedback loop becomes unstable. This lesson explains precisely when. The method is to watch where the closed-loop poles go as the gain changes. It also covers the reverse case, which is the reason control engineering exists at all: using feedback to make an **unstable** system stable.

## The closed-loop poles are new poles

From lesson 1.4, a controller $C(s)$ and a plant $G(s)$ in a feedback loop have the closed-loop transfer function

$$\frac{Y}{R} = \frac{CG}{1 + CG}$$

Its poles are the roots of the denominator:

$$1 + C(s)\,G(s) = 0$$

This is the **characteristic equation** of the loop. Its roots are not the poles of the plant. They depend on the controller, and that is the whole point: **changing the controller gains moves the poles.** Stability is decided by where they end up, with the same rule as before: every pole must have a negative real part.

## Example 1: a loop that is always stable

Take a motor position plant with P control:

$$G(s) = \frac{50}{s(0.2s + 1)}, \qquad C(s) = K_p$$

The characteristic equation is $0.2s^2 + s + 50K_p = 0$. As $K_p$ rises from zero, the two poles, which start at $0$ and $-5$, move toward each other along the real axis, meet at $-2.5$ when $K_p = 0.025$, and then split into a complex pair that travels straight up and down:

| $K_p$ | Closed-loop poles | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | $-0.56,\ -4.44$ | overdamped, slow |
| 0.025 | $-2.5,\ -2.5$ | critically damped |
| 0.1 | $-2.5 \pm 4.3j$ | $\zeta = 0.5$ |
| 1.0 | $-2.5 \pm 15.6j$ | $\zeta = 0.16$ |

The real part stays at $-2.5$ however large the gain becomes. The loop never goes unstable, but its damping ratio falls toward zero, so it rings more and more. A plot of pole positions as a gain varies is called a **root locus**.

## Example 2: one more pole changes everything

Real hardware always has extra lag somewhere: the motor driver, the electrical time constant, a sensor filter. Add a small one, a 20 ms lag:

$$G(s) = \frac{50}{s(0.2s+1)(0.02s+1)}$$

The characteristic equation is now a cubic, $0.004s^3 + 0.22s^2 + s + 50K_p = 0$. This time the complex pair does not go straight up. It bends to the **right**:

| $K_p$ | Rightmost poles | |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | $-2.23 \pm 4.5j$ | stable |
| 0.5 | $-1.25 \pm 10.8j$ | stable, poorly damped |
| 1.0 | $-0.19 \pm 15.1j$ | barely stable |
| 1.2 | $+0.19 \pm 16.5j$ | **unstable** |

At $K_p = 1.1$ the pair sits exactly on the imaginary axis and the loop oscillates forever at 15.8 rad/s. That gain is the *ultimate gain* $K_u$ from lesson 2.3, and $2\pi/15.8 = 0.397$ s is the period $T_u$. Beyond it the oscillation grows.

A lag of only 20 ms, ten times faster than the motor, turned a loop with unlimited gain into one with a hard limit. This is the general lesson: **it is the fast dynamics you left out of the model that determine how much gain you can use.**

## A test without solving for the roots

For a cubic there is a simple algebraic stability test, a case of the *Routh–Hurwitz criterion*. The polynomial

$$a_3 s^3 + a_2 s^2 + a_1 s + a_0$$

has all roots in the left half-plane if and only if all four coefficients are positive **and**

$$a_2\,a_1 > a_3\,a_0$$

Apply it to example 2: $0.22 \times 1 > 0.004 \times 50K_p$ gives $K_p < 1.1$, the limit found numerically.

It also settles a question left open in lesson 2.2. With PI control, the arm's characteristic equation is $Is^3 + bs^2 + K_p s + K_i = 0$. The test demands $bK_p > IK_i$. With $b = 0.2$, $K_p = 20$, $I = 0.108$ and $K_i = 40$: is $4.0 > 4.32$? No. The loop is unstable, which is why the PI-controlled arm never settled. Add the D term and $b$ becomes $b + K_d = 2.2$: now $44 > 4.32$, comfortably stable. The test tells you in one line how much integral gain a given amount of damping can support.

## Stabilizing an unstable plant

So far feedback has been a threat to stability. Now use it the other way.

Point the arm straight **up**. From lesson 1.6, linearizing about the upright position with $\delta$ the small angle from vertical gives

$$I\ddot{\delta} + b\dot{\delta} - mgd\,\delta = u$$

The minus sign is the trouble: gravity pushes the arm *away* from vertical, like a spring with negative stiffness. The open-loop poles are at $-6.2$ and $+4.4$. The positive one means any disturbance grows like $e^{4.4t}$.

Apply PD control, $u = -K_p\delta - K_d\dot{\delta}$. The characteristic equation becomes

$$Is^2 + (b + K_d)\,s + (K_p - mgd) = 0$$

For a quadratic, stability needs all coefficients positive, so the condition is simply

$$K_p > mgd = 2.94\ \text{N·m/rad}$$

The controller's spring must be stiffer than gravity's negative spring. Below that threshold nothing else helps; above it the arm balances, and $K_d$ sets how well damped it is. The lab confirms it:

| $K_p$ | $K_d$ | Closed-loop poles | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | $-6.2,\ +4.4$ | falls |
| 2 | 0.5 | $-7.6,\ +1.1$ | falls, more slowly |
| 4 | 0 | $-0.9 \pm 3.0j$ | balances, with ringing |
| 4 | 0.5 | $-4.1,\ -2.4$ | balances |
| 20 | 2 | $-10.2 \pm 7.4j$ | balances firmly |

Note the second row. A gain that is too small does not partly stabilize the arm; one pole is still in the right half-plane, and the arm falls. Stability is a yes-or-no property.

This is the first milestone of the course: a system that cannot stand by itself is held upright by a few lines of feedback, and you can predict from a polynomial exactly which gains will work.

=== frc ===

Three familiar FRC problems are this lesson. An arm that oscillates harder as you raise $k_P$ is example 2: the encoder's velocity filtering and CAN delay are the extra pole, and they set your ultimate gain. An inverted arm, or a robot balancing on a charge station, is the unstable plant: there is a minimum gain below which it simply falls. And the reason the roboRIO's 20 ms loop is fine for an elevator but motor controllers run their current loops at 1 kHz or more is that the faster the plant's dynamics, the less delay the loop can tolerate before the poles cross to the right.

=== summary ===

- Closed-loop poles are the roots of $1 + C(s)G(s) = 0$. Gains move them.
- A two-pole plant under P control never goes unstable but loses damping as gain rises.
- Any extra lag bends the root locus into the right half-plane, giving a maximum gain $K_u$.
- Cubic $a_3s^3 + a_2s^2 + a_1s + a_0$: stable ⇔ all coefficients positive and $a_2a_1 > a_3a_0$.
- An unstable plant can be stabilized by feedback, but only above a minimum gain. For the upright arm, $K_p > mgd$.
